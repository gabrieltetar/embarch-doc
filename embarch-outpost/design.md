# embarch-outpost: design

**Status: working end to end on real hardware as of 2026-08-27 — a study captures a real nRF54L15's thread/ISR/GPIO timeline and the UI renders it named and timed. Record layout 3.** Milestone 7 Phase E is closed. [gabrieltetar/embarch-outpost](https://github.com/gabrieltetar/embarch-outpost) carries the module, its manifest generator, and its tests (Milestone 7 Phase C, [milestone-1.md](milestone-1.md) §4). It builds for `native_sim` and for the real nRF54L15 reference-dut target; a `native_sim` run produces a stream that decodes cleanly (41 frames, 941 records, every record kind including gaps, no CRC or framing failures); and the generator resolves 13 real ISRs and 5 real threads out of the reference-dut's own ELF. The host halves in [embarch-core](../embarch-core/design.md) and [embarch-api](../embarch-api/design.md) shipped in the same pass, since Phase C emitting a real manifest was their named trigger.

**The DUT carries a clock again, and it costs no locks — layout 3, 2026-08-27 (§3 decision 4's reversal).** A record is `{cycles, kind, a, b}`. Layout 2 had removed the timestamp outright, on the correct observation that reading `k_cycle_get_32()` inside the context switch and inside `_isr_wrapper()` is the instrument charging its cost to the path it measures. **The objection was to the lock, not to the clock.** On a GRTC-timed nRF that call resolves to `sys_clock_cycle_get_32()`, which takes a `k_spin_lock` around `nrfx_grtc_syscounter_get()`, which takes a *second* critical section around a 64-bit read in a retry loop — all to return 32 bits that sit in the SYSCOUNTER's low word. `src/outpost_time.h` reads that word directly. So the emit path is lock-free and every record still has a time.

A rendered row now carries **both** clocks, named for what they are: `frame_index,frame_seq,rx_utc_ms,cycles,us,kind,a,b,name`. The DUT's `cycles` orders and measures events *within* a frame; the host's `rx_utc_ms` places the trace against every other stream in a study. Neither substitutes for the other, and layout 1 (DUT columns only) and layout 2 (host columns only) were each narrower than they needed to be.

**The version is 3 and not 1, although the wire is byte-for-byte what 1 was.** A version byte exists so a host can say "I decode up to N"; a number reused after a different, mutually-unreadable wire has already worn a higher one cannot say that — two incompatible streams would both announce `1`, separable only by which build produced them, which is the one thing the byte is there to settle. [embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 80.

**Bytes have now crossed a real UART, and the first thing they said was not about wire constants (2026-08-27).**

What works, end to end and measured rather than reasoned: the reference-dut builds a flashable outpost image (`-S ble-shell -S outpost`), its generated `outpost-manifest.json` declares `record_layout_version: 3` with real ELF reads (5 threads, 12 ISRs, 24 devices, 3186 functions), `embarch-core` accepts the flash, the `outpost` signal is declared as a `Route::Direct` on the trace adapter, Core resolves it and opens the port, and a study with a `StreamSource::Signal` tap runs to completion and serves the tap. **355 bytes carrying four COBS frame delimiters arrived on that adapter after a flash-and-reset — the first outpost bytes ever on a real wire.**

**And then it went quiet for a day, across three diagnoses, because nobody had checked which board was on the desk (2026-08-27, [embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) rows 83–85).**

The stream stopped after 355 bytes. The first diagnosis was a *DUT-side emission stall*; the second, after reading the running silicon over SWD, was *the emitter is fine and the wire is missing*. Both were wrong, and the second was wrong in an especially expensive way — it was extremely well evidenced. UARTE20 read back `ENABLE=8`, `BAUDRATE=0x03B00000` (230400), `PSEL.TXD=0x20`, `DMA.TX.AMOUNT == MAXCNT`; the module's own record ring, decoded out of RAM, showed a ~4.4 ms cycle whose UARTE ISR spanned 4157 µs — what 92 bytes takes at that baud. The instrument was measuring itself emitting at 91% of line rate. A control build with no snippets at all, console on the same UART at 115200, was equally invisible on all five of the host's COM ports, with a positive control on the same scan proving the listener worked.

Every one of those measurements was correct. The conclusion drawn from them was not, because they all shared one unexamined premise: **the board being built was `roadrunner@2`, and the board on the bench is `dut_dev` revision 7.** `roadrunner`'s variant-less target puts console uart20 on P1.00; `dut_dev@7` puts it on **P1.05** (schematic `DBG_RX` — the nets are crossed on rev 6 and 7). The trace was being transmitted, perfectly, into a pin with nothing attached to it.

**The tree already contained the answer, twice.** `dut_dev_npm1304_common.dtsi` describes the exact symptom, from a previous bring-up: *"transmitting (TXDRDY / ENDTX / TXSTOPPED all fire, DMA AMOUNT correct) — the byte simply lands on the wrong pad. Swapping TX<->RX here makes the console work. Confirmed on hardware."* And this snippet's own header block has said `-b dut_dev@7/nrf54l15/cpuapp` since it was written. The wrong board came from `embarch-api`'s `config.toml`, whose board field was "corrected" on 2026-08-06 by reading the reference-dut repo's stale `build/build_info.yml` — a fact derived from an artifact and then written down as authoritative. See [embarch-study-designer/design.md](../embarch-study-designer/design.md) §3 decision 35: *which board is on the bench* is exactly the class of fact this suite forbids inferring, and it was inferred anyway, by the tool that exists to enforce the rule.

**What it looks like when it works.** Rebuilt for `dut_dev@7`, flashed, and the trace appeared at once — **437,789 bytes in 20 s, 21.9 KB/s**, decoding to 43,948 records: 10,481 ISR entries across ten named vectors (`uarte_nrfx_isr_async`, `nrfx_isr(nrfx_spim_21_irq_handler)`, `rtc0_nrf5_isr`, `swi_lll_nrf5_isr`, the GPIOTE and TWIM handlers), named application threads (`meas_scheduler_tid`, `g_alive_tid`, `batchmgr_result_thread`, `main`), and 648 `gpio_dispatch`/`gpio_callback_done` pairs — layout 3's new records, on real hardware, first time out. A study then captured **92,724 bytes** on its `outpost` tap, and the Trace view rendered it `named: true, timed: true`: 9205 rows, 766 frames, 24 lanes, `records_lost: 0`, `unstamped_rows: 0`, 4.0 ms resolution over a 4069 ms window.

**The trace is demo-quality as of 2026-08-27, and getting there took five levers and overturned two of the assumptions behind them.**

The number the previous session left to be uncomfortable about was that the outpost's own drain thread accounted for **78% of the reference capture's measured extent**. It never did: that figure was `embarch-ui` timing spans by frame arrival while the wire had been carrying a per-record microsecond clock since layout 3 landed the day before. On the DUT's own clock the drain thread is **1.6%** of the window (66.3 ms over 778 runs, 85 µs each), every ISR in the system together is **0.6%**, and the reference-dut is **95.6% idle** ([embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 86).

The real problem was the **wire**, and it was worse than a share of anything: **2256 records/s → ~22.9 KB/s against 230400's 23040 B/s ceiling, 99% duty with the DUT otherwise idle**, and 50.4% of records were the instrument describing its own transmission. Where it ended up, measured on `dut_dev@7` over a 40-second capture:

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| link duty | **99%** | **27%** |
| records that are the instrument | **50.4%** | **0%** |
| records that are the application | 18.6% | 100% of what is left |
| bytes per record | 10.1 | **9.84** |
| records per frame | 12.0 | **20.7** |
| records lost | 405 under light load | **0** unstimulated; still 19.7% in bursts under the demo's load (§7) |
| threads resolved to a name | **5 of 20** | **20 of 20** |
| spans the host could measure | 669 of 4955 | **all of them**, to 1 µs |

**What got it there, and what each lever actually did** — §3 decisions 17, 19, 20 and 8, and §7's wire-constants table:

1. **The host clock** (decision 17). Not a wire change at all: `embarch-ui` and `embarch-core` were reading the coarser of the two clocks the wire carries. Fixing it moved the resolution from 4.0 ms to 1 µs and took 4286 unmeasurable spans to zero, without touching the DUT.
2. **Self-exclusion** (decision 19). Took the instrument out of its own trace: 50.4% of records to 0%. **It did not move the duty cycle** — 99% to 96% — which is the finding, not the failure.
3. **Pacing** (decision 20). The drain loop was a fixed point at the link rate: it sent, blocked on the transmit, and drained exactly what arrived meanwhile, settling at **3.3 records per frame** with ~9 bytes of framing on each. That is why removing half the records changed nothing. Waiting for the batch to fill took frames to 20.7 records and the duty cycle to 37%. **Only possible because layout 3 gave every record its own timestamp** — under layout 2 a delayed frame would have moved every event in it.
4. **Baud, 230400 → 460800** (§7), which is the ceiling of this link and not a preference: the MCP2200 bridge refuses to open above 750000 and the nRF54L15 has no baud between 460800 and 921600.
5. **Naming** (decision 8) and **ring depth**. DWARF type joined to symbol-table address resolves 20 of 20 thread objects where symbol names resolved 5; `_irq_vector_table` names the one `IRQ_DIRECT_CONNECT` handler `_sw_isr_table` cannot, which on this board is the radio. Raising the ring from 128 to 512 slots closed the last 43 dropped records — a burst, not a rate, and exactly what decision 20's own Kconfig help predicts the fill wait will provoke.

**What is left, and it is one number:** a record costs **9.92 bytes**, which puts the drain thread's *peak* throughput at ~4600 records/s — and the demo capture's bursts go past that, losing 19.7% of their records in three honestly-drawn gap bands while the link averages 36% busy. Every cheaper lever is already in and the ring cannot grow further on this DUT (2048 slots is 40 KB against a build at 81.75% of 256 KB). The two largest contributors to those 9.92 bytes are both absolute 32-bit values in five-byte varints — `cycles`, and `a` when it is a RAM pointer. §3 decision 4 rejected a per-record delta on `cycles` **for a reason that was entirely about emit-path cost, not wire cost**; that reasoning is still correct and no longer relevant. It is a layout-4 change and it is **not** made here — see §7.

## 1. Purpose and scope

`embarch-outpost` is **the first EmbArch component that ships inside the thing under test.** Every other sub-project observes a DUT from outside it — Core through a debug probe, dev-bench over the air, `embarch-api` over HTTP. The outpost is a small Zephyr module an engineer compiles into their *own* DUT firmware, for debug builds, that emits a running account of what the MCU is actually doing — which thread ran, in what order, when it was in interrupt context, and whatever application-specific spans the engineer chose to mark — out a dedicated TX-only UART, to be recorded and rendered host-side.

The question it answers is the one no external instrument in this suite can: **"the study passed, but what was the CPU doing while it did?"** A `Study` today can tell you a GATT write produced a notification 40 ms later. It cannot tell you whether those 40 ms were one busy thread, forty context switches, or an ISR storm.

**And it answers that question at frame resolution, not at cycle resolution** — layout 2's deliberate trade (§3 decisions 4, 17). For the 40 ms above it will tell you which threads ran, in what order, how many switches and how many ISR entries there were, and roughly where in those 40 ms each frame's worth of activity fell. It will **not** tell you that a particular ISR took 12 µs. "One busy thread, forty context switches, or an ISR storm" is exactly the question it still answers; "how long did this one interrupt take" is the question it gave up.

**Why this is not a violation of the no-inference rule.** [embarch-study-designer/design.md](../embarch-study-designer/design.md) §3 decision 35 forbids any EmbArch component presenting an inference about what a specific piece of hardware or firmware does as established fact. The outpost is the structural opposite of an inference: the engineer compiles it in, chooses what to mark, and ships a build-time manifest declaring what every ID means. Nothing here reads a DUT's source and guesses. It is the explicit engineer-supplied-knowledge pipeline that rule implies should exist, applied to timing rather than to GATT semantics.

**v1 scope, explicitly bounded:**

- **TX-only.** The DUT talks; nothing talks back. No host commands, no runtime enable/disable, no `CONFIG_TRACING_HANDLE_HOST_CMD` equivalent. The protocol is designed so a receive direction can be added later without a reshape (§4), but there is no RX path, no flow control, and no acknowledgement of any kind in v1.
- **Study-scoped capture, rendered post-hoc.** Core opens the outpost link for the duration of a `Study` and closes it after. There is no always-on mode and **no live feed** — settled explicitly (§3 decision 10), against the original framing of this work, which asked for realtime. The timeline is drawn from the recorded stream once the study completes.
- **Zephyr only.** One vendor-neutral shared core is *not* being designed up front; this is a Zephyr module using Zephyr's own `sys_trace_*` hook contract (§3 decision 2). A bare-metal or other-RTOS port would need a real porting layer that does not exist and is not being speculatively designed.
- **No power, no GPIO, no stimulus.** The outpost observes its own MCU. It does not sample, drive, or measure anything.
- **No clock.** Nothing in the module reads a timer, per record or per frame (§3 decision 4). It reports *what happened and in what order*; when it happened is the host's answer, at frame granularity.

`embarch-outpost` is **not**:
- A production feature. It is a debug build's instrumentation, sized and defaulted accordingly.
- A logging library. The DUT's own `printk`/`LOG_*` output is a different stream with a different shape, and pointing the outpost at it would make the two indistinguishable. A DUT console is a legitimate *second* signal under §3 decision 11's routing model — it is not this one.
- A replacement for a debugger, an ETM trace probe, or SystemView. It is deliberately the cheap, wire-thin version: one UART pin, no probe bandwidth, no vendor tool.

## 2. Architecture overview

```
        DUT firmware (engineer's own repo, debug build)

        │    sys_trace_k_thread_switched_in/out()  ──┐   │   CONFIG_TRACING_USER
        │    sys_trace_isr_enter/exit()             ├──▶ │   hooks — outpost
        │    sys_trace_idle()                       │   │   implements them
        │                                           │   │
        │  application code                         │   │
        │    OUTPOST_EVT(PPG_FRAME_BEGIN, n)  ──────┘   │   engineer-placed
        │                                               │   markers, build-

        │      lock-free record ring  (outpost-owned)   │
        │              ▼                                │
        │      drain thread → COBS frames → uart_tx()   │   async/DMA, TX-only
        └───────────────────────┬────────────────────────┘
                                │  outpost UART, TX only
                                │
             ┌──────────────────┴───────────────────┐
             │                                      │
       route = direct                        route = via-dev-bench
       (today — "dev-bench bypass")          (not yet possible: no hardware)
             │                                      │
             ▼                                      ▼
    USB-UART bridge on the                 dev-bench RX pin ──▶ byte
    Core machine                           pass-through over its existing
             │                             link — dev-bench never interprets
             │                                      │
             └──────────────────┬───────────────────┘
                                ▼
                          embarch-core
              opens the tap for the study's duration, writes
              streams/outpost.bin verbatim, **stamps every frame
              with its own receipt time** into
              streams/outpost.arrival.csv, and decodes both
              against the build-time manifest into
              streams/outpost.trace.csv
                                │
                                ▼
                          embarch-ui — timeline, post-hoc
```

Three properties carry the design:

1. **The route is a bench fact, not a study fact.** A `Study` names the *signal* (`outpost`); [embarch-topology](../embarch-topology/design.md) resolves which carrier currently delivers it. The same study runs unchanged before and after the pass-through hardware exists (§3 decisions 11, 12).
2. **Nothing between the DUT and Core interprets a byte.** Core decodes, against a manifest the DUT's own build produced. Whichever carrier is in use moves bytes and stamps nothing but arrival.
3. **The clock is Core's, and the DUT has none** (decision 4, reworked 2026-08-26). No record carries a timestamp; Core stamps each *frame* as it arrives. That makes a frame the resolution of the whole instrument — ordering inside one is real, duration inside one is not measurable — and it makes the trace share an axis with every other stream in the study, which the DUT's own cycle counter never could.

## 3. Locked-in design decisions and rationale

1. **The outpost is a Zephyr module (C), consumed by west, compiled into the DUT's own firmware — not a library the DUT links, not a sidecar image, not something Core flashes separately.** The DUT firmware repo adds it to its west manifest and turns it on with Kconfig. This matches how `embarch-dev-bench` already consumes `embarch-study-designer` (a west-managed project, that doc's §3 decision 8) and is the only shape that can see a kernel hook at all — the instrumentation must be in the same image as the kernel it is instrumenting. §5 has the whole integration path concretely — what the engineer adds, where, and what each thing is allowed to decide.

    **Every board-specific fact is declared in the DUT's own repo, never here.** Which UART peripheral, which pins, what baud, how big the ring is: all of it is a devicetree `chosen` node plus Kconfig in the consuming project (§5). That is not just tidiness — an outpost that shipped an opinion about which UART instance to use on an nRF54L15 would be asserting a fact about someone else's board that it is in no position to know, and would be wrong for the second consumer. The module ships mechanism and defaults; the DUT repo declares the facts.

   *Rejected: a vendor-neutral portable C core with a porting layer.* Genuinely wider reach, and genuinely more design for a reach nothing currently needs — both real DUT firmware repos in play are Zephyr. Revisit when a non-Zephyr DUT is real, not before.

2. **Trace source: `CONFIG_TRACING_USER`'s kernel hooks plus engineer-placed `OUTPOST_EVT` markers — not CTF, not SystemView, not Percepio, and not a statistical sampler.** Zephyr already calls a fixed set of hooks at exactly the points that answer "what is the CPU doing": `sys_trace_k_thread_switched_in/out`, `sys_trace_isr_enter/exit`, `sys_trace_idle`, plus thread create/abort/suspend/resume/name-set/priority-set/ready/pend. The outpost implements them. No engineer instrumentation is required to get a thread timeline.

   **Verified, not assumed** (`zephyr/subsys/tracing/`), because three things about `TRACING_USER` materially shape everything below and none of them are obvious:
   - **`CONFIG_TRACING_USER` does not select `TRACING_CORE`** (`subsys/tracing/Kconfig`, `CMakeLists.txt`). Zephyr's tracing ring buffer, its sync/async format layer, its drain thread, its backends, and its drop counter are **all** compiled out. The outpost owns the entire emit path — which is why decisions 3/4/5 exist at all, and why they are not "replace Zephyr's backend."
   - **`sys_trace_named_event(name, arg0, arg1)` is an empty macro under `TRACING_USER`** (`subsys/tracing/user/tracing_user.h:447`). Zephyr's own manual-marker API does nothing here. Markers must come from us (decision 6).
   - **`sys_trace_isr_enter()` and `sys_trace_k_thread_switched_in()` take no arguments.** Which ISR, and which thread, must be recovered inside the hook — see decisions 7 and 8.

   *Rejected: `CONFIG_TRACING_CTF`.* CTF is the standard, decodable-by-babeltrace choice, and it was the leading option until the wire cost was checked: every CTF thread event carries an inline `ctf_bounded_string_t` — `CTF_MAX_STRING_LEN` is **20** (`ctf/ctf_top.h:17`) — so a thread switch is roughly 29 bytes rather than roughly 8. On a UART, at the switch rates that make a load trace interesting, that is the difference between a stream that fits and one that spends its life dropping. CTF also carries a real, undocumented-here timestamp hazard: `CTF_EVENT` stamps `k_cyc_to_ns_floor64(k_cycle_get_32())` **truncated to `uint32_t` nanoseconds**, which wraps every ~4.29 seconds regardless of clock rate (`ctf/ctf_top.h:52-60`). Rejecting CTF costs tool compatibility and buys a wire the DUT can actually sustain.

   *Rejected: SEGGER SystemView.* Requires RTT over the debug probe, which is a second carrier this suite would then have to model, and is a vendor tool with its own host application — the opposite of "one UART pin, rendered in `embarch-ui`."

   *Rejected: a periodic statistical sampler, and polled `k_thread_runtime_stats`.* Both are cheaper and both answer a smaller question. A sampler cannot see a short ISR; runtime stats give a per-window percentage with no timeline at all. The hooks give an exact timeline for less firmware than a sampler needs.

3. **The outpost owns its own transport: a lock-free record ring, a low-priority drain thread, and the asynchronous UART API (`uart_tx()`/DMA where the SoC has it).** Forced by decision 2 — with `TRACING_CORE` compiled out there is nothing to reuse — but it is also the right answer independently. **Zephyr's stock UART tracing backend writes with `uart_poll_out()`, one byte at a time, in a loop** (`subsys/tracing/tracing_backend_uart.c:70`), and additionally `depends on UART_CONSOLE`. Spinning per byte inside the drain path burns exactly the CPU time the trace is trying to measure. The outpost's own UART instance is a dedicated devicetree node, distinct from the DUT's console.

   **Built 2026-08-25, and the "lock-free" in this decision survived contact with the implementation** — which was not a foregone conclusion, since a spinlock is genuinely cheaper here in the common case. The ring is a Vyukov-style bounded MPSC queue: a producer CASes a monotonic `head` to reserve a slot, writes it, then publishes by storing the reservation number into the slot's own sequence, and the consumer advances only over slots whose sequence matches the index it is reading. A producer preempted mid-write stalls the drain at that slot rather than letting a later record overtake an earlier one.

   *Rejected: a `k_spinlock`.* On a uniprocessor Cortex-M that is an `irq_lock` around ~10 instructions, which is cheap and obviously correct. It is not used for a reason specific to what the emit path *is*: it runs **inside interrupt context at arbitrary priority**, and an `irq_lock` there raises the interrupt latency of every other ISR in the system by the length of the trace write. A trace whose cost is a latency floor on unrelated interrupts distorts exactly the thing it is measuring. The CAS loop charges the producer, not the system.

   One implementation detail worth recording because it is not obvious and it is load-bearing: a slot holding reservation `n` publishes `n + 1`, never `n`. That offset is what makes the ring correct straight out of BSS with **no initialiser to run** — and there is no moment at which running one would be safe, because the first hooks fire during kernel startup, long before any `SYS_INIT` the module could register. Zero-initialised memory would otherwise read as "reservation 0 is published" to a consumer sitting at tail 0, in exactly the window that matters.

   **The `uart_poll_out()` fallback exists** for a port whose driver has no async support, behind `CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST_UART_ASYNC` — present so such a port still produces a stream, not because it is fine. `native_sim`'s own PTY UART does support the async API, so the end-to-end test exercises the real path.

4. **Wire format: compact fixed-shape records, postcard-encoded, COBS-framed in batches — carrying no timestamp at all.** The emit path from an ISR is: write one record into the ring, return. No clock is read anywhere in the module. The drain thread does all framing. Records carry IDs, never strings (decision 9 is what makes IDs sufficient).

   **Reworked 2026-08-26, at the repo owner's call, on emit-path cost** — replacing "an absolute 32-bit cycle count in every record", which is what layout 1 shipped. The reasoning that killed it: `k_cycle_get_32()` was being read *inside the context switch and inside `_isr_wrapper()`*, at arbitrary interrupt priority, which is the instrument charging its cost to the exact path it exists to measure. On a Cortex-M that read is a memory-mapped timer access, not a register move, and it was the only unbounded-cost operation left in the hook (decision 3 had already refused an `irq_lock` there for the same class of reason — a trace whose cost is a latency floor on unrelated interrupts distorts what it measures). Nothing about this was measured on hardware; it is a design judgement about where the instrument's cost is allowed to land, made by the person who owns the DUT.

   **What replaces it: the host's own receipt time, per frame** (decision 17). Core already stamps every chunk it reads with `rx_utc_ms` and always has (`embarch-core/design.md` §3 decision 30); for an outpost tap it used to throw that away. Now it keeps it, and a record's time is the arrival stamp of the frame that carried it.

   **What it bought, measured on the same `native_sim` workload:** a record went from 12 bytes to 7 worst-case and **9.25 → 6.32 bytes per record** actual, including framing and header frames; 941 records got out where 848 did before, with one gap record instead of four. A ring slot went from 20 bytes to exactly 16, which is the constant `CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST_RING_BYTES` is divided by — so every ring had quietly been 1.25× its configured size, and the unit test asserting `slots * OUTPOST_SLOT_BYTES <= RING_BYTES` passed the whole time, because it measures the product through that same constant. A `BUILD_ASSERT` was added on the equality. **Both were lost when layout 2 was withdrawn** — see the changelog's 2026-08-27 ring-budget entry; layout 3 restored `cycles`, a slot is 20 bytes again, and the constant and the assert had to be put back a second time. The header lost its `cycles_per_sec` field, the manifest lost its `cycles_per_sec_config` field, and the host lost the cycle unwrapper entirely — with it, the whole failure mode of [embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 40.

   ~~**What it cost, stated plainly rather than in §7.**~~ **What it cost while layout 2 was on the wire, and what got that cost back.** Layout 2's price was that a frame became the resolution of the instrument: two records in one frame were ordered and *simultaneous as far as the capture knew*, and a span that opened and closed inside one frame had no measurable duration — **389 of 941 records' spans**, and every ISR span, on the committed `native_sim` capture. Layout 3 restored `cycles`, which took that price back in full: on the reference reference-dut capture **0 of 4955 spans** are below the resolution, and the resolution is 1 µs rather than 4 ms (decision 17's table).

   **The host did not follow for a day, and that is the more useful half of this paragraph.** `embarch-ui` was written against layout 2 and kept timing every span by its frames' arrival stamps after layout 3 landed, so it reported the outpost's own drain thread at **78% of the capture** where the DUT's own clock says **1.6%**, and reported 4286 spans as unmeasurable when every one of them was measurable. Nothing was wrong with the wire; the host was reading the older of the two clocks it carried. Fixed 2026-08-27 — [embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 86 — and the general shape is worth keeping: **a wire change is not done when the DUT emits it, it is done when every host that decodes it has been re-measured against it.**

   *Rejected: a per-record delta varint.* ~1 byte a record instead of 5, and — computed at drain time, between consecutive records *in the ring* — a dropped record just widens one delta, so the timeline stays correct, which is the objection layout 1's own text raised and got wrong. It was declined anyway because it does not address the stated problem: the emit path still reads the counter, which is the cost being removed.

   *Rejected: one cycle stamp per frame, read by the drain thread.* This one genuinely answers the emit-path cost — the read moves out of the hooks and into a thread, five bytes per ~256-byte frame — and it was on the table and declined. Declined because it buys no resolution: intra-frame timing is gone either way, so all it adds is a second clock in the system, with a rate field, an unwrap, and a DUT-cycles-to-host-time fit to keep honest. One clock, coarse and shared with every other stream, beat two clocks at the same resolution.

   *Rejected: keeping the per-record stamp behind a Kconfig.* Two wire layouts to decode, two sets of host arithmetic, and a `*.trace.csv` whose columns depend on a DUT's build options — for a choice this repo has no measurement to make either way yet. When there is one, the layout version is the mechanism for changing it again.

5. **Overflow policy: drop, count, and emit an explicit gap record. Never block, never overwrite.** When the ring is full the record is dropped and a counter increments; the next records frame opens with a gap record naming how many were lost. **The host renders the gap as a gap** — a labelled band in the timeline — rather than drawing a continuous, plausible, wrong picture across it.

   Drop-the-newest rather than overwrite-oldest: overwriting discards the beginning of a busy burst and keeps the aftermath, which is backwards for a continuous timeline. Zephyr's own tracing increments a drop counter that never reaches the wire; closing that is this module's job, because the losses correlate with load, which is exactly when the trace matters.

   **The gap record's position is now load-bearing, and it is all there is.** Through layout 1 a gap carried the cycle span its losses fell across, stamped when they started — which made it the one record whose timestamp could precede the records printed after it, and which broke the host's unwrap on the first real capture ([embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 40, 99 timestamps thrown forward by 2^32). Decision 4 removed every timestamp, so `b` is 0 and reserved, and what bounds the losses instead is a **position guarantee: a gap record is always the first record of its frame** (emitted directly into the frame, never through the ring — the ring being full is the reason it exists). A host therefore bounds the losses between the previous frame's arrival and that frame's: **a bound of one frame, presented as a bound.**

   Two honest consequences of that: the band is *wider* than one frame whenever a header frame arrived in between, because a rendered trace carries only frames that held records and cannot see the header's arrival — too wide is still a bound, too narrow would be a claim. And row 40's failure mode is gone outright: with no timestamps there is no unwrap, and no backwards step to misread as a wrap.


6. **Manual markers: `OUTPOST_EVT(ID, arg)` with build-registered IDs — and the same registration generates the manifest.** The engineer declares their markers in one place (an X-macro list or a linker-section registration, settled at implementation); that declaration is simultaneously what makes `OUTPOST_EVT(PPG_FRAME_BEGIN, n)` compile and what puts `PPG_FRAME_BEGIN` in the manifest. An unregistered ID is a **build error**, not a mystery integer on the host. No strings cross the wire.

   **Keeping that table in the image is a real problem, not a formality** ([embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 41). *Nothing in the firmware reads it* — the generator reads it out of the ELF — so `-fdata-sections --gc-sections` deletes it and **the build still succeeds**, producing a manifest with zero markers and a trace whose markers are bare integers, with no error anywhere to say why. Two mechanisms were tried before the one that works. `zephyr_iterable_section()` looks like exactly the right API and has none: it is built on the CMake linker-script *generator*, and Cortex-M and POSIX both use the template-`.ld` path. A `zephyr_linker_sources(RODATA ...)` `KEEP()` does survive the Zephyr link — and is collected out again by the *second* link, the host link that produces `zephyr.exe` on a native build. What holds on every target is a `volatile` pointer to the table, read once at init: a relocation from a section the compiler may not elide.

   Forced into existence by decision 2's verified finding that `sys_trace_named_event` is a no-op under `TRACING_USER`. Deliberately **not** implemented by also overriding `sys_trace_named_event` for compatibility with DUT code already using the stock API: that API takes a runtime `const char *`, which means either interning strings on the DUT or putting them on the wire, and both re-introduce exactly the cost decision 2 rejected CTF over. A DUT wanting outpost markers writes outpost markers.

7. **ISR identity: the outpost reads the active vector number in the hook and emits it raw; the manifest resolves it to a handler name from the ELF's own `_sw_isr_table`. Engineer markers stay, for spans *inside* a handler.**

    **Reversed 2026-08-25, same session it was written**, after checking the mechanism against a real Zephyr checkout instead of reasoning about it. The original decision rejected reading the vector register on two grounds — that it is arch-specific code in a module claiming to be portable Zephyr C, and that turning an exception number into a handler name is "a second inference step over the vector table, which is the kind of derived-and-presented-as-fact answer this suite has already been burned by." **The second ground was wrong, and it was the load-bearing one.** Both halves check out as facts, not inferences:

    - **Reading the number is what Zephyr itself does, at the same instant.** `sys_trace_isr_enter()` is called from **inside `_isr_wrapper()`** (`arch/arm/core/cortex_m/isr_wrapper.c:33-37`), in exception context, and three lines later the same function computes `int32_t irq_number = __get_IPSR(); irq_number -= 16;`. Evaluating that expression in the hook is not a heuristic that usually works — it is the identical expression, guaranteed valid at that point, returning the number the wrapper is about to dispatch on. IPSR is still the same exception at `sys_trace_isr_exit()`, so the number is emitted on exit too, which makes nesting and tail-chaining trivial for the host instead of a stack it has to reconstruct.
    - **Resolving the number to a name is an ELF read, not a guess.** `_sw_isr_table[]` is "an array of these structures **indexed by the irq line**" (`include/zephyr/sw_isr_table.h`), each entry `{ const void *arg; void (*isr)(const void *); }`, and `const` unless `CONFIG_DYNAMIC_INTERRUPTS`. The manifest generator walks it at exactly the index the firmware will report and resolves the function pointer against the symbol table — **the same mechanism decision 8 already uses for `_k_thread_obj_*`**, applied to a different table. Nothing is derived, nothing is matched heuristically, and the number on the wire stays raw regardless of whether a name was found.

    So the honest split is not "number vs. name" — it is **"what the kernel dispatches" vs. "what happens inside a handler."** The first is now free and exact. The second is what markers are for: an `OUTPOST_EVT` inside a handler marks a span the kernel has no visibility into, and no vector number can substitute for it. Both, not either.

    **What this genuinely costs, stated rather than buried.** `__get_IPSR()` is Cortex-M; a `CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST_ISR_IDENTIFY` Kconfig gates it, on by default where the arch supports it and off elsewhere, degrading to the anonymous enter/exit records the hooks give regardless. `CONFIG_ARM_CUSTOM_INTERRUPT_CONTROLLER` builds substitute `z_soc_irq_get_active()` for IPSR in the wrapper — a real variant, explicitly unsupported in v1 rather than assumed equivalent, and the symbol now `depends on` its absence so such a build degrades rather than reporting a plausible wrong number. `CONFIG_DYNAMIC_INTERRUPTS` makes `_sw_isr_table` runtime-filled, so ELF entries may still be `z_irq_spurious` placeholders and those IRQs render as bare numbers. Multi-level interrupt aggregators (`CONFIG_2ND_LVL_ISR_TBL_OFFSET`) put second-level IRQs at a table offset, which the generator must honour or mislabel — named here because mislabelling is the one outcome decision 9 exists to prevent. And the timestamp is wrapper-entry, not handler-entry: a fixed prologue's worth of cycles early, consistently, for every ISR.

    **Checked against the reference-dut's real configuration, 2026-08-25 — none of those three is present, and a fourth thing this decision never named defeats most of its IRQs anyway** ([embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 42). The `dut_dev@7/nrf54l15/cpuapp` build has `CONFIG_DYNAMIC_INTERRUPTS` unset, `CONFIG_MULTI_LEVEL_INTERRUPTS` unset (so no aggregator offsets), `ARM_CUSTOM_INTERRUPT_CONTROLLER` not a symbol at all, `CONFIG_GEN_SW_ISR_TABLE=y`, and `CONFIG_NUM_IRQS=271`. Every precondition holds.

    What does not hold is the assumption that the table's `isr` field names something useful. **Nordic's nrfx glue registers one shared `nrfx_isr` trampoline per peripheral and passes the driver's real handler as the entry's `arg`**, so 8 of the reference-dut's 13 populated entries resolve to the same useless name. The fix is one field over and is the same kind of read: resolve `arg` against the symbol table too, and take it when it points at a *function*. That recovers `nrfx_uarte_0_irq_handler`, `nrfx_grtc_irq_handler`, `nrfx_saadc_irq_handler`, `nrfx_twim_30_irq_handler` and the rest, which the manifest carries as `isr_args` alongside `isrs`. `CONFIG_SHARED_INTERRUPTS` is a fifth variant of the same shape — a `z_shared_isr` dispatcher in the table position — unset here, and named now rather than found later.

8. **Thread names come from the build-time manifest, by DWARF *type* joined to symbol-table *address*; anything not covered renders as a raw pointer.** `sys_trace_k_thread_switched_in()` takes no arguments, so the hook reads the current thread pointer — a runtime address, four bytes on the wire, no name. What turns it into a name is an address → name table generated into the manifest at zero wire cost, alongside the marker IDs.

   **Reworked 2026-08-27, and the sentence this decision used to end on is what changed.** It said: *"a thread created with `k_thread_create()` into a plain `static struct k_thread` has no distinguishing symbol prefix, and identifying it would need DWARF type inspection. Those threads render as raw pointers."* That was an accurate description of a limit and a wrong judgement about its size. On the reference-dut the name match resolved **5 of 20** thread objects, and the fifteen it missed are most of what a quiet trace actually does: `drain_thread` (the outpost's own, 778 runs in the reference capture), `bt_long_wq` and `bt_workq` (the BLE stack's work queues, 12 ms a run), `k_sys_work_q`, `prio_recv_thread_data`, `recv_thread_data`, `app_sm_thread_data`, `shell_uart_thread`, and four application work queues. **Four of them are not even a `struct k_thread`** — a `struct k_work_q` starts with one, so a work queue's address *is* its thread's address, and that is the pointer `k_thread_create()` hands the kernel. No naming convention could have found any of them.

   So the generator does the DWARF inspection the old text named and declined. **DWARF says what a variable is; the symbol table says where it lives; neither is guessed**, and both are facts the build recorded — the same standard every other table in the manifest is held to. Two details that are load-bearing rather than incidental:

   - **Type from DWARF, address from the symbol table.** Only definition DIEs carry a location, and Zephyr's own kernel objects — `z_main_thread`, `z_idle_threads`, `k_sys_work_q`, `_thread_dummy` — appear in this image *only* as `DW_AT_declaration` DIEs: typed, with no address. Insisting on a DWARF location loses exactly the four objects a quiet trace spends most of its time in. A name is enough to join on, and the join is exact.
   - **First-member recursion, at offset 0 only.** A member at any non-zero offset stops it, because then the outer object's address is not the thread's.

   An array of threads gets one entry per element at the element type's own recorded `DW_AT_byte_size` — which is what finally names `z_idle_threads` per CPU, and retires the old note about a stride "the symbol table does not state". The `_k_thread_obj_*` name match survives as the **fallback** for an image linked without debug info, and which path ran is in the manifest's `notes`; a `K_THREAD_DEFINE` the type walk somehow missed is added from the name match and the disagreement is reported rather than papered over.

   **What is still not covered:** an image with no DWARF resolves the 5 it always did. And a thread whose `struct k_thread` is a member of something at a non-zero offset is not found — no build in this suite has one, and finding it would mean claiming an address the kernel was never handed.

   Still chosen over emitting runtime name-registration records, which would cover every thread and put strings back on the wire.

   **The same pass closed the equivalent gap on the ISR side.** `_sw_isr_table` holds only the vectors that dispatch through `_isr_wrapper()`; an `IRQ_DIRECT_CONNECT` line leaves its row empty and resolved to nothing. On this target exactly one line does that, and it is **the radio** — the single most interesting vector in a BLE trace. `_irq_vector_table[irq]` is a function address under `CONFIG_IRQ_VECTOR_TABLE_JUMP_BY_ADDRESS`, so any entry that is not `_isr_wrapper` is a direct handler; comparing against `_isr_wrapper`'s own symbol is what makes that exact rather than a guess about which entries are interesting. IRQ 138 is now `radio_nrf5_isr`.

9. **The manifest is a build artifact, CRC-matched against the running firmware, and a mismatch refuses to decode rather than decoding wrong.** The DUT build emits `outpost-manifest.json` — schema version, marker IDs → names, thread addresses → names, record layout version, and a CRC over the whole thing. The firmware emits that same CRC in its stream header record. Core stores the manifest with the study and **will not render a trace whose header CRC does not match the manifest it was given**; it records the raw stream and reports the mismatch.

    This is the whole reason IDs on the wire are acceptable (decisions 6, 8). It is also the failure mode that must be loud: a stale manifest against a rebuilt firmware would silently relabel every marker, producing a trace that is entirely readable and entirely wrong — the worst possible outcome and exactly the class of staleness [embarch-topology](../embarch-topology/design.md) was created to eliminate. The manifest travels to Core the same way the firmware artifact already does, through `embarch-api`'s build step (§5.4, and §7 for the remote-Core gap this inherits).

    **Selection and verification are two questions, answered by two mechanisms — and the post-link CRC patch this decision originally specified was over-engineering, corrected 2026-08-25 the same session.** "Which manifest should I decode this against?" and "is this actually the right one?" are not the same question, and trying to make one exact mechanism answer both is what drove the original design into patching a CRC into the linked image.

    **Selection: the study's own flash binds it.** The manifest belongs to the build the study just put on the DUT — same operation, so there is no interval in which the binding can rot. **This is not the write-ahead-staleness pattern [embarch-topology/design.md](../embarch-topology/design.md) §3 decision 3 exists to eliminate, and the distinction is exactly where that principle's edge lies**: what decision 3 forbids is a *persisted* record of resolved state, consulted at some later, unrelated moment. A binding whose lifetime is the study that created it has no later moment to be wrong in. The original framing of this decision rejected "Core remembers what it flashed" without making that distinction, and was wrong to.

    **Resolved 2026-08-25 by [embarch-study-designer/design.md](../embarch-study-designer/design.md) §3 decision 40**, which is what supplies the flash this selection depends on: a `Study` declares the DUT firmware version it requires, and the operator chooses per run whether to reflash to satisfy it. **Reflash is optional, which matters here** — an outpost trace captured on a run that did *not* reflash falls back entirely to the build-ID check below, and its `StudyResult.provenance` says so (`Declared` rather than `FlashedThisRun`). Note this does **not** retire `embarch-umbrella`'s doctor check 13, as an earlier draft of this entry claimed: a per-study gate only fires when a study runs, while check 13 answers "is this bench current" before anything is attempted at all. The two are complementary, not redundant.

    **Verification: a compile-time build ID in the header frame.** `git describe --dirty` on the DUT repo, the outpost module's own pinned revision (§5.1), and a hash of the marker registration list — all knowable at compile time, so this is a generated header rather than an image-patching post-link step. Core compares it against the manifest's copy and refuses to render on a mismatch. On the real reference-dut build that string is `v1.2.1-2-g81bb829-dirty+op0909fda-dirty+md41d8cd9`.

    **The `manifest_crc` this doc kept describing in two other places could never have existed, found 2026-08-25 by producing the first real manifest** ([embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 37). §4's header record listed one, and `embarch-study-designer`'s `StreamEncoding::OutpostTrace { manifest_crc }` had **shipped** one in Phase A. Both were survivors of the post-link CRC patch this very decision had already replaced: the manifest is generated *from the linked image* — its thread and ISR tables are ELF reads — so no CRC of it exists at compile time for the firmware to carry. The tap variant was doubly wrong, since a CRC chosen when a study was *authored* is precisely the persisted-and-later-consulted record this decision spent three paragraphs distinguishing itself from; a saved study's copy would go stale on the next rebuild. `OutpostTrace` is now a unit variant, §4's header carries the build ID instead, and which manifest applies is a Core-side runtime question. The manifest still carries a `manifest_crc` of its own — a content fingerprint for telling two manifests apart, computed and read entirely host-side, never compared against anything the firmware says.

    **The route it travels settled 2026-08-25, and it is not a route of its own: an optional `manifest` part on `POST /flash`'s existing `multipart/form-data` body, plus an optional `manifest_path` field on its JSON body** — a sibling of `firmware`/`firmware_path` on the call that already carries the artifact (`embarch-core/design.md` §3 decision 30, Settlement 1). This decision said the manifest "travels to Core the same way the firmware artifact already does" without naming a shape; making it literally the same request is what makes the selection rule above hold with no machinery: the manifest and the image it describes arrive in **one operation**, so there is no interval in which Core holds one without the other and no "which manifest is current" record to keep. A separate `POST /manifests` would have needed exactly that record — the pattern this decision spent three paragraphs distinguishing itself from.

    **Built 2026-08-25/26 — the trigger fired.** Phase C emits a real `outpost-manifest.json`, so [embarch-core](../embarch-core/design.md) §3 decision 30(c)'s four mechanisms and [embarch-api](../embarch-api/design.md) §3 decision 39's pickup-and-send both shipped in the same pass, as their shared trigger always said they would. What that turned into concretely: `POST /flash` takes an optional `manifest` part / `manifest_path` field; Core holds one manifest **per chip**, replaced by a flash that carries one and **cleared** by a flash of the same chip that does not; a study snapshots that binding into its own results directory at start; and the trace is decoded post-hoc against it, with a build-ID mismatch costing the *names* rather than the capture — the rows are still written, and `streams/index.json` carries a note saying why they are unnamed. `embarch-api`'s half derives the manifest path from the artifact's own directory rather than taking it as a parameter, because "the engineer never handles that file" reads the same way for a caller: a parameter is a thing a dozen call sites can forget.

    The paragraph below is kept as the record of why it was *not* built for a day, since the trigger mechanism is the thing worth preserving, not the delay.

    **Not built, and closed with a named trigger rather than left as owed work.** Storage, selection, verification and refuse-to-render are four mechanisms and nothing can exercise any of them yet: no build emits a manifest until Phase C's generator exists, no header record exists to check a build ID against, and no `Route::Direct` is physically real until Phase E's bridge is. **The trigger is Phase C emitting a real `outpost-manifest.json` from a real ELF** — a single precondition, since that is also what makes a header record exist. **`embarch-api`'s half is deferred under the identical trigger, settled 2026-08-25** ([embarch-api/design.md](../embarch-api/design.md) §3 decision 39, Milestone 7 Phase B item 2): a sender and a receiver for the same manifest are useless apart, so one trigger governs both rather than each half waiting on the other. What *is* live today is this decision's failure behavior, reached from the other side: `embarch-core` writes an `OutpostTrace` tap's raw bytes and **renders nothing**, because it has no manifest and will not guess one. Same outcome, arrived at by having nothing rather than by having the wrong thing.

    **Each mechanism covers precisely the other's blind spot**, which is why both and not either. Flash-binding is exact through a dirty-tree rebuild — the common case during active debugging, and the one a `-dirty` build ID cannot distinguish. The build ID catches a DUT flashed out-of-band between the study's flash and its capture — a bare `west flash` or an IDE button, entirely normal, and the case flash-binding is blind to. Neither alone is sufficient; together they cover what the post-link CRC patch covered, without the build machinery.

    **What is genuinely given up**, stated rather than glossed: a DUT flashed out-of-band from a *dirty* tree that happens to carry the same `-dirty` string still decodes against the wrong manifest. That is the one residual hole, it is narrow, and it is the price of not patching the image. If it ever bites for real, the post-link CRC stamp is the known fix and this entry is the record of why it wasn't paid for up front.

    The same header record makes a `doctor`-style staleness check possible for the DUT, mirroring `embarch-umbrella`'s existing check 13 for dev-bench firmware ([embarch-dev-bench/design.md](../embarch-dev-bench/design.md) §3 decision 25): the running firmware reports its outpost version and manifest CRC, and that can be compared against the module revision currently checked out. Not built here; named because the mechanism is now free.

10. **Capture is study-scoped and rendered post-hoc. There is no live feed.** Core opens the outpost tap when a `Study` starts and closes it when the study ends; the timeline is drawn from the recorded stream afterwards.

    **This reverses the framing this work opened with** — "debug MCU load in realtime and see what the CPU is doing over time" — and the reversal was made deliberately, by the user, when the two halves were put side by side. It is worth recording why rather than letting the doc quietly disagree with its own motivation. A live feed costs a decode-and-push path in Core, an SSE channel, and a renderer that can draw a partial timeline, all before the first byte has ever been captured — and the actual debugging question ("what was the CPU doing during that study") is answered just as well by a complete recording as by a partial live one. Always-on capture is a plausible later addition; it is not a v1 requirement, and pretending it was would have shaped the port-ownership design around a case nobody asked to use yet.

11. **"Dev-bench bypass" is not an outpost feature — it is a general routing property of a new topology entity, the DUT signal link.** [embarch-topology/design.md](../embarch-topology/design.md) §3 decision 18 introduces it: a named signal originating at the DUT, with a declared **route** — either `direct` (straight to the Core machine, bypassing dev-bench entirely) or `via-dev-bench { pins }`. The outpost UART is the first instance; a DUT console UART, an SWO pin, and eventually a dev-bench-driven stimulus line are the obvious next ones.

    **Today's route is `direct`, for a stated hardware reason, not a design preference.** The intended topology is DUT → dev-bench → Core, because dev-bench is what already owns a validated link to Core and what will eventually correlate a trace against its own BLE view. The bench does not currently have the pins or the pass-through firmware to do it, so the outpost's UART goes to a standalone USB-UART bridge on the Core machine instead. Modelling that as a first-class declared route — rather than as "the temporary way it happens to be wired" — is what makes the eventual move a declaration change instead of a redesign, and what makes the topology diagram able to draw an honest picture of a signal that skips a node.

    **When the route is `via-dev-bench`, dev-bench passes bytes through and interprets nothing** — receives on the declared pin, stamps arrival, forwards. Not decode-and-summarise: putting outpost record semantics inside dev-bench firmware would make dev-bench carry knowledge of a specific DUT's build, which decision 35 rules out and which would also mean reflashing the bench whenever the DUT's manifest changes.

12. **A `Study` names the signal; topology resolves the carrier.** The tap says `outpost`, not "COM7" and not "dev-bench pin 4". This is what makes decision 11 pay: the identical saved study — and saved studies are now a real, versioned artifact ([embarch-study-designer/design.md](../embarch-study-designer/design.md) §3 decision 38) — runs unchanged before and after the bench gains pass-through hardware, on a machine whose bridge enumerates differently, or against a Core running elsewhere. The alternative, naming the concrete source in the study, is more explicit on the wire and re-authors every saved study the day the bench is rewired.

13. **The outpost's capture is a `StreamTap` — which means [embarch-stream-pipeline-proposal.md](../embarch-stream-pipeline-proposal.md)'s inbound half is accepted, and `embarch-study-designer` decision 36's dedicated-variant route is reversed one day after it shipped.** See [embarch-study-designer/design.md](../embarch-study-designer/design.md) §3 decision 39 for the folded-in types and [embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) for the reversal row.

    **The collision was flagged before this was decided, and the decision was made anyway, knowingly.** Decision 36 (2026-08-25) deliberately chose a dedicated `DevBenchMessage::GattTranscriptRecord` plus `gatt.csv` over generalising `StreamChannel`, reasoning correctly that `Sample`'s single `f32` has no room for raw bytes, a direction, or a UUID pair. That reasoning was an argument against *the shape `StreamChannel` had*, not against a generic pipeline — and the proposal's tap model was designed around exactly that objection. The cost is real and is not being minimised: a schema bump (written here as 5 → 6; it landed 2026-08-25 as 7 → 8, since decisions 42/43 were implemented first), a second wire-contract pinning pass in both C and Rust, and edits to firmware that is code-complete and deployed. The offsetting fact, which is what made it affordable: **Milestone 6's dev-bench firmware has not been flashed or run against hardware yet**, so nothing about decision 36's wire shape is load-bearing on a real bench. This is the last moment it is cheap, which is the same argument the proposal made for itself in §1 — it just now applies to `gatt.csv` too.

16. **The outpost's UART reaches the Core machine over the DUT board's own USB — there is no separate bridge, and none gets bought, 2026-08-25.** §7 carried this as an open hardware pick, and it was the wrong question.     This dissolves the hazard §7 was most worried about. There is no third device: the DUT board is one the bench already has and already identifies. The `SignalLink` still declares a `port_serial`; it just names a port that already exists.

The large half is that this decision's own premise is false: the DUT's UART is **not** wired to a USB connector plugged into the PC.

    Established by construction, not by absence of output. A build with `CONFIG_SHELL=y`/`CONFIG_UART_CONSOLE=y` was flashed and **proved to be running** — dev-bench connected to it over BLE and read back its real GATT table — and then every COM port on the machine was watched across a hardware reset. `COM5` is the *same* J-Link attached to this DUT (its VCOM reports probe serial `000852006107`), so the debug connector does carry a virtual COM port — and it returned **zero bytes**, meaning that VCOM is not wired to `uart20`. `COM4` is a genuine Microchip MCP2200 USB-UART bridge and returned only `0x00` at 9600/115200/230400/1M, which is a line held low rather than a baud mismatch. The chip transmits; nothing listens.

    So `Route::Direct` remains the right *model* and has still never existed physically. What Phase E starts with is exactly the wiring task this bullet hoped to rule out: a bridge's RX on **P1.05** — not P1.04, whose `DBG_TX` schematic label is on a net physically crossed on both rev 6 and rev 7 — and a ground. [milestone-1.md](milestone-1.md) §5 had predicted this outright ("needs hardware that does not exist yet: a USB-UART bridge … and a wire"), and this decision is what talked the plan out of believing it.


17. **Two clocks, and each one has exactly one job: the DUT's `cycles` measures, the host's `rx_utc_ms` places. Reworked 2026-08-27.**

    Written 2026-08-26, when decision 4 had removed every DUT timestamp and host receipt time was the trace's *only* clock. Layout 3 restored `cycles` the next day at no lock cost, and the sentence that has to change is "only": both clocks are on the wire, they answer different questions, and **neither substitutes for the other.**

    - **`cycles`, read per record by `outpost_time.h`, is what measures.** A span's duration is the difference between its two ends' own stamps. On this target that is microsecond-exact.
    - **`rx_utc_ms`, stamped per frame by `embarch-core` (`embarch-core/design.md` §3 decision 30), is what places.** It is the same wall clock `core_rx_utc_ms` carries on a power sample and on a GATT transcript entry, so laying a trace beside a power capture is an alignment rather than a guess. Layout 1 had no sync point between DUT cycles and host wall time at all, and nothing in the suite ever bridged them; that gap closed on 2026-08-26 and stays closed.

    **The rules that follow, in the order a host applies them:**

    - **A host measures on the finest clock every row carries, and says which one it used.** `embarch-ui` has three tiers — DUT microseconds, host milliseconds, frame index — and reports the axis clock by name (`embarch-ui/design.md` §3 decision 10). One row missing the stamp its tier needs drops the whole view to the next tier down: half a timeline in microseconds and half in milliseconds is one axis pretending to be another, and so is half in milliseconds and half in frame indices.
    - **Nothing interpolates, on any clock.** Even spacing inside a frame would look better and be fabricated.
    - **A frame is the resolution only when the host's clock is the one drawing the axis.** With `cycles` present, which frame delivered a record is a fact about the transport and has no bearing on how long anything took.
    - **What is below the resolution is a property of the clock, not of the wire.** On the host's clock a span whose two ends arrive in one frame has no measurable duration, is counted, and contributes zero — and an ISR is below the resolution *by construction* there, because enter and exit land in one frame essentially always. On the DUT's clock nothing is below the resolution, and an ISR is timed like anything else.

    **Measured, and the numbers are why this decision was reworked rather than annotated.** The same reference capture (`bbd4818b`, 9205 rows, 4.08 s of a quiet reference-dut), summarised on each clock:

    | | host clock | DUT clock |
    |---|---|---|
    | resolution | 4.0 ms | 1 µs |
    | spans below it | **4286 of 4955** | **0** |
    | `drain_thread` share | **78.1%** | **1.6%** (66.3 ms over 778 runs, 85 µs each) |
    | idle thread | 7.9% | **95.6%** |
    | every ISR in the system | 147 ms, almost all excluded | **24.8 ms = 0.6%**, all measured |
    | `uarte_nrfx_isr_async` | 2 spans measurable of 1540 | 1540 of 1540, **9.9 µs each** |
    | window unaccounted for | 430 ms (10.6%) | 4.3 ms (0.1%) |

    The 78% is the mechanism of the host clock, stated plainly: the drain thread switches in on one frame and out on the next, so it is charged the whole frame interval — 3.9 ms for 85 µs of work, 46× over. A load table built on that clock does not report the instrument's cost, it reports the instrument's *framing*.

    *Rejected: interpolating records evenly across a frame's interval.* One line of code, a visibly smoother chart, and a lie in exactly the register this suite exists to refuse — it manufactures a resolution the wire does not have, and nothing downstream could tell the manufactured part from the measured part. Still rejected, and now unnecessary: the resolution it would have manufactured is one the wire actually has.

    *Rejected: reconstructing intra-frame timing from the record count and the baud rate.* "N records took N×12 bytes at 1 Mbaud, so they were 96 µs apart" describes when the bytes *left*, not when the events *happened*: the ring decouples them, which is the whole point of having a ring.

    *Rejected: dropping `rx_utc_ms` from the rendered row now that `cycles` measures better.* It is the only thing that places this trace against another stream in the same study, which is a job the DUT's counter cannot do at all — there is no sync point between them. `embarch-ui` keeps reporting the frame interval as the *placement* resolution while measuring in microseconds, which is the pair of facts a reader needs.

    **The DUT's clock is the only one of the three that can go backwards, and it does so for two unrelated reasons — added 2026-08-27, from a real capture.**

    - **Microseconds, inherently.** A hook reads the counter and *then* reserves its ring slot, so an interrupt that preempts a thread between those two operations reserves after it and is stamped before it. The ring publishes in reservation order, so the pair emerges inverted by the width of that window. A real study's capture showed one such step, of **13 µs**. This is not a defect and a host must tolerate it — refusing the clock over it would refuse every real capture. It also means the earliest instant in a capture is not reliably in its first row, so a window must be taken from the minimum.
    - **Seconds, meaning the counter restarted.** A capture spanning a DUT reset holds two epochs and timing anything across the boundary is meaningless. The same study showed a step of **195 seconds**, and its effect was not subtle: an 1.2-second capture's window read as 11 ms and two lanes came out with a **9159% share**.

    **What separates them is the other clock, not a threshold.** A backwards step longer than the whole capture took is two independent clocks contradicting each other, and the host's is monotonic — so that is the test, and with no host stamps the DUT's own total forward span stands in. When it fires, `embarch-ui` refuses the DUT clock, falls to the host's, and says so; `dut_backsteps` and `dut_backstep_max_us` are reported either way so the harmless inversions stay visible.

    **The cause of the 195-second step was Core, and it is fixed at the source.** A TX-only DUT transmits whether or not anyone is listening, so the OS driver's receive buffer can already hold minutes-old bytes when a study opens the port. Core now discards them on open (`embarch-core/design.md` §3 decision 30's settlement 3). Worth recording *why nobody had seen it*: on a monotonic host clock a stale prefix is invisible — it just looks like a capture that started earlier. **Making the DUT's clock the axis is what made a pre-existing defect observable**, which is the second time in two days that decision 17's rework has surfaced something that was always wrong.

    *Rejected: letting a host pick its clock per span.* Measure the long spans on the DUT's clock and the short ones on the host's, or vice versa — a mixed axis, arrived at one span at a time, and unauditable. The tier is chosen once for a whole view and named.

18. **The arrival stamps are persisted beside the capture, keyed by frame index, and a join that cannot be verified stamps nothing. 2026-08-26.** Decision 10 renders post-hoc, from the complete raw file, long after the read that saw the bytes — so the stamps have to survive that gap on disk. `streams/<tap>.arrival.csv` does it: `frame_index,rx_utc_ms,frame_bytes`, one row per frame, written incrementally as the bytes arrive.

    **The key is a frame index both sides count the same way, and neither side decodes for the other.** Core's capture path counts non-empty runs between `0x00` delimiters; the decoder counts exactly the same thing (`outpost::chunks`'s enumeration order). A frame that later fails its CRC still consumed an index on both sides, which is what keeps them in step — the alternative, indexing by *successfully decoded* frame, silently shifts every stamp after the first corrupt frame.

    **`frame_bytes` is in the row so the join is checkable.** The raw capture rotates under retention (`embarch-core/design.md` §3 decision 30's segment rotation) and this file deliberately does not, so their "frame 0" can stop being the same frame. Each row carrying its frame's length means the renderer can try the two alignments that can actually occur — the log starting where the capture starts, and the log running ahead of a capture that lost its beginning — and **verify** the one it picks. When neither fits, nothing is stamped and `streams/index.json` says why. A whole trace shifted by three frames is readable, wrong, and indistinguishable from a correct one; refusing is the same posture decision 9 takes toward a manifest from another build.

    **`named` and `timed` are two facts in the index, not one note.** A trace can be named and untimed, timed and unnamed, or neither, and `note` is prose for a person. Before this, `embarch-api`'s client derived "is this trace fully resolved?" from `rendered && note.is_none()` — correct while a note could only ever mean "unnamed", and wrong the moment an untimed-but-named trace could carry one. Both booleans now come from Core's own finding, over `GET /study/{id}/streams`, and no caller pattern-matches on the prose.

19. **The outpost keeps itself out of its own trace, by default, and says so on the wire. 2026-08-27.** `CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST_TRACE_SELF`, default **n**: no record is emitted for a context switch into or out of the drain thread, or for an ISR enter/exit on the vector of the UART named by the `embarch,outpost-uart` chosen node.

    **The measurement that forced it.** On a quiet `dut_dev@7` the steady state was a closed loop of exactly ten records per frame, and every one of the ten was caused by transmitting the previous frame:

    ```
    thread_switch_in idle / idle / isr_enter uarte / isr_exit uarte (17 µs)
    thread_switch_out idle / thread_switch_in drain_thread
    isr_enter uarte / isr_exit uarte (3 µs) / thread_switch_out drain_thread / thread_switch_in idle
    ```

    Of the reference capture's 9205 records, **50.4% were the drain thread plus its own UART's ISR** (IRQ 198, confirmed as uart20 out of the devicetree — `interrupts = <0xc6 0x1>`) and **18.6% were the application**, at 99% of line rate with the DUT doing nothing. A frame carried almost exactly the story of its own transmission. Measured after the change: **0.0% self, 68.3% application.**

    **Default n, and that is a deliberate reversal of this module's usual posture.** Everywhere else the outpost defaults to reporting more rather than less. Here the default *removes* information, because the information removed is almost never what anyone wants and its cost is the whole link. `y` is the honest-but-expensive setting and it is exactly right when the thing being debugged **is** the outpost.

    **The header says which, and the flag's clear state is the interesting one.** `OUTPOST_FLAG_TRACE_SELF` is set only under `y`. It is on the wire because **an absence of records is indistinguishable from an idle subject**: a host that inferred self-exclusion from "the drain thread never ran" would be deriving a firmware build option from a measurement, which is precisely the class of thing [embarch-study-designer/design.md](../embarch-study-designer/design.md) §3 decision 35 forbids. Core reads the bit onto `streams/index.json`'s `self_excluded`, beside `named` and `timed` — a third independent way a trace can be incomplete, and the only one the *firmware* decides.

    **What a host sees instead of the drain thread's run** is an interval no lane covers, which lands in the load summary's unaccounted total — 1.6% of the reference window. `embarch-ui` renders the flag against exactly that number, because that is the number it explains.

    **`thread_create` and `thread_name` are not excluded**, deliberately. They are two records for a whole capture rather than two per frame, and between them they are the only thing that names the excluded subject. A self-excluded trace that also hid the drain thread's existence would leave a host with unattributed intervals and nothing to attribute them to.

    **Two limits, stated rather than discovered later.** The ISR half excludes the whole **vector**, so under `CONFIG_SHARED_INTERRUPTS` anything else sharing it goes too. And it needs `DT_IRQN` to equal the Cortex-M vector number, so it compiles out under `CONFIG_MULTI_LEVEL_INTERRUPTS` rather than comparing `__get_IPSR() - 16` against an encoded number that does not match it — the same posture `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_ISR_IDENTIFY`'s own `depends on` already takes.

    *Rejected: excluding the idle churn the drain thread causes.* With the drain thread gone, what remains of the loop is the idle thread switching out and back in. Those records are caused by the instrument and are **not** the instrument: the idle thread really did run. Excluding them would be the trace lying about the CPU rather than declining to describe itself.

    *Rejected: coalescing the drain thread's run into one "the instrument ran here" record.* One record instead of six, and it would close the hole in the timeline honestly. Declined for now because it is a new record kind, three host decoders and a layout bump for something the header flag plus the unaccounted total already communicates — revisit if the hole turns out to confuse a reader in practice.

    **What it did not do, and this is the more useful half.** Self-exclusion removed half the records and **did not move the link's duty cycle at all** — 99% before, 96% after. See decision 20: the drain loop's shape, not its record count, is what sets the duty cycle, and cutting records made frames smaller rather than rarer.

20. **The drain thread waits for a batch to fill, and that is only free because the DUT kept its clock. 2026-08-27.** `CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST_FILL_WAIT_MS`, default **10**: if the ring holds less than a batch's worth, sleep once, then send whatever is there. `0` restores the original send-immediately behaviour.

    **The loop was a fixed point at the link rate, and nothing about record volume could escape it.** `drain_thread_fn` sends, blocks on the transmit, and then drains exactly the records that arrived while it was blocked — so frame size settles at *whatever the record rate produces during one frame's own wire time*, and duty sits near 100% however few records there are. Removing records makes frames smaller, which makes them transmit faster, which makes the loop spin faster. That is why decision 19 cut half the records and moved the duty cycle by three points.

    Measured at 460800 with the instrument already excluded: **6063 frames in 6 s, 3.3 records each, 42.8 bytes each.** About nine of those bytes are frame type, seq, count, CRC-32, COBS stuffing and the delimiter, so **roughly a fifth of the link was carrying no records at all** and a record cost 13.0 bytes against a body of about 9.5. After the wait: **20.2 records per frame, 200.6 bytes per frame, 9.95 bytes per record** — and the duty cycle finally moved, 94% → 37%.

    **The trade is latency, and only latency — which is exactly what decision 4's rework bought back.** A record can now sit in the ring up to `FILL_WAIT_MS` before it is transmitted. That costs nothing because a record carries its own DUT timestamp again (decisions 4, 17), so *when it left the ring has no bearing on when the host says it happened*. **Under layout 2 this change would have been unavailable**: a frame's arrival time was the only clock, so delaying a frame would have moved every event in it. The same is true of resolution — bigger frames used to mean a coarser trace and now mean nothing of the kind. This is the clearest case yet of decision 17's rework paying for something other than accuracy.

    One sleep and not a loop, because a second wait would double the latency bound to buy a second-order saving. The target is computed against `OUTPOST_RECORD_MAX_BYTES` rather than an average, so it is reached before a batch could overflow and a frame is never cut short by having waited for one record too many.

    `outpost_ring_pending()` is documented as a **hint, not a fact**: it can be stale, and it can count a slot a preempted producer has reserved but not published. Both errors make the drain thread wait *less* than it meant to, which costs a slightly smaller frame and nothing else. It is clamped to the ring size, because a producer that reserved past a full ring increments head and then drops its record, so head can run ahead of anything a consumer will ever see.

    **What the wait does not affect is burst tolerance, and the ring is what does — measured 2026-08-27.** During a burst `outpost_ring_pending()` is far above the batch target, so the drain thread never sleeps and its throughput is identical to the wait being 0. The wait costs latency and only latency. What bounds a burst is the drain thread's **peak** throughput — a 256-byte frame every 5.75 ms of wire time at 460800, about **4500 records/s** — against the ring's depth.

    That distinction had to be measured to be believed. On an unstimulated board at 1700 rec/s the losses were zero; a study running `GattMonitorAll` (a full GATT discovery plus a subscription to every characteristic) lost **6962 records across 48 gaps in 38 seconds while the link averaged 24% busy**. Average capacity was never the constraint. Raising the ring from 512 to 2048 slots took it to **8036 records across 5 gaps** — the ring absorbed 43 of the 48 overflow events, and the five that remained are bursts far past 4500 rec/s. So: **the ring is the burst knob, the wait is the latency knob, and the record's size is the throughput knob** — and only the third one is still unturned (§7).

    *Rejected: a smaller `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_BATCH_BYTES` to get finer frames.* This was a live idea while the frame was the instrument's resolution, and decision 17's rework inverted it: frame size no longer affects resolution at all, so smaller frames now buy nothing and cost framing overhead. **Bigger is strictly better on this wire**, up to the latency the wait already bounds.

21. **`<embarch/outpost.h>` is includable in a build that does not have the module, and `OUTPOST_EVT()` compiles to nothing there — 2026-08-27.** A one-line CMake fix for a defect that made decision 6's markers unusable in exactly the code they are most worth putting in.

    The header was already written for this: its markers-compiled-out path no-ops, and its own comment says an unregistered name is a build error *"whether or not markers are enabled — the failure mode this is arranged to prevent does not depend on a Kconfig."* That design was defeated by this module's `CMakeLists.txt`, not by the header. `zephyr_include_directories(include)` sat **below** the `if(NOT CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST) return()` guard, so `#include <embarch/outpost.h>` did not resolve at all in a build without the module. The no-op path could never be reached from an application, and the compile-time name check could never run in the build where a typo costs the most — the shipping one.

    **The cost landed exactly where markers earn their keep.** A marker is worth most in a hot path: a driver's drain loop, a queue's overflow branch, a library's error return — code compiled into both the tracing image and the shipping one. Reaching any of those meant the application wrapping every call site in its own `#ifdef CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST` and hand-rolling the fallback macro the header already contained. Found placing the first real `OUTPOST_EVT()` markers into a DUT's PPG pipeline, where the alternative was adding guards to four files of someone else's product code to work around a header that had the answer built in.

    Two changes. The include directory moves above the guard — it adds no sources, no symbols and no code, since with `CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST` unset the header's entire content is an enum and two macros that expand to nothing. And the no-op degrades in **three** tiers rather than two, described in §5.2: the third tier drops the name check, because with no registration header included there is no enumerator to check against, and `(void)OUTPOST_MARKER_##id` there would fail every call site rather than catch anything.

    `tests/module_off` pins it: a compile-only application that includes the header and calls `OUTPOST_EVT` with `CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST=n`. It is in `tests/run-all.sh` alongside the ztest, stream and cross-decoder stages.

    **Validated on real hardware the same day**, which is also the first time this module's markers ran on a DUT at all: five markers declared at a PPG pipeline's sample-drop sites, all five resolved by name in `outpost-manifest.json`, and three `PPG_PULL_DROPPED` records decoded out of a study capture ~100 ms apart — the observe-poll cadence — each carrying the engineer's own argument. Decision 6 shipped 2026-08-26 and had until now only ever been exercised by this repo's own test app.


## 4. Wire format

**Implemented and pinned; record layout 2 as of 2026-08-26.** The specification of record is `embarch-outpost/src/outpost_priv.h`, with three implementations that must agree: the firmware encoder, `embarch-study-designer`'s `outpost.rs` (which `embarch-core` and `embarch-ui` read a trace through, for the same reason `Sample`'s row shape lives there — Core holds no column knowledge), and `embarch-outpost/scripts/decode_outpost.py`. Every constant below is still provisional until the first real capture over a real UART (§7); each is a Kconfig (§5.3).

**Record** — what the emit path writes into the ring. **No timestamp** (§3 decision 4):

```
{ kind:   u8,         // ThreadSwitchIn | ThreadSwitchOut | IsrEnter | IsrExit
                      // | Idle | ThreadCreate | ThreadName | Marker | Gap
  a:      u32,        // kind-dependent: thread pointer, vector number,
                      //   marker ID, dropped count
  b:      u32 }       // kind-dependent: marker arg; 0 where unused
```

postcard-encoded, so each `u32` is a varint. A thread switch is **7 bytes** worst case and 6 typical, against 12 under layout 1; measured over the whole committed capture, framing and header frames included, a record costs **6.32 bytes** where it used to cost 9.25. A ring slot is **20 bytes** under layout 3 — `cycles` is back — which is the number `CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST_RING_BYTES` is divided by, and a `BUILD_ASSERT` pins the equality so the two cannot drift again. Layout 1 had 20 against a constant that said 16, so every ring was 1.25× its configured size; layout 2 made the constant true by shrinking the slot; layout 3 made it false again the other way and the guard did not survive the merge. The default 4096 now buys 128 slots and 2560 bytes.

**Layout 1 is not decoded by anything, deliberately.** Its records opened with a five-byte varint timestamp, so a layout-2 decoder pointed at a layout-1 stream would read each timestamp's first byte as a kind and produce plausible, wrong rows for the whole capture. The version byte in the header is what makes that a refusal instead — the same mechanism, and the same posture, as a manifest from another build.

**Frame**:

```
frame := COBS(body || crc32_ieee(body) as 4 bytes LE) || 0x00
body  := frame_type: u8, seq: u8, payload
```

- `frame_type` **0x01 — Records.** The payload is a postcard `Vec<OutpostRecord>`: a varint count then that many records. Batches are capped at 127 records so the count stays one byte. **A gap record, when there is one, is always the first record of the frame** — that position is what bounds the losses in time (§3 decision 5).
- `frame_type` **0x02 — Header.** Emitted at startup and every `CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST_HEADER_INTERVAL_MS`, so a host attaching mid-stream can decode: `record_layout_version: u8`, `flags: u8`, `outpost_version: string`, `build_id: string`. Layout 1 also carried `cycles_per_sec` here; there is no DUT clock left for it to describe, and a rate reported beside the host's own timestamps would only invite arithmetic across two clocks.

**The header is a frame type, not a record kind, settled at implementation.** This section originally listed `Header` among the record kinds, and its fields do not fit the fixed record shape. Making it its own frame type also pays off for the case it exists for: a host attaching mid-stream finds a header at a COBS boundary without decoding a single record.

**It carries a build ID and no manifest CRC** — see §3 decision 9. The two strings are the only strings on this wire, at most one header a second, and they are what make the manifest check possible at all; the rule decision 2 rejected CTF over is a rule about *per-record* cost, and this is not that.

`flags` says which hook families the running firmware actually has compiled in — threads, ISRs, idle, markers, ISR identification, blocking overflow — so a host never infers that from the absence of records.

**Framing**: COBS, the same convention the Core⟷dev-bench link already uses ([embarch-study-designer/design.md](../embarch-study-designer/design.md) §3 decision 10), byte-for-byte the same routine, so Core sees one framing convention on both of its links. The CRC covers the body only, sealed before COBS, so a host that finds a delimiter early checks the same bytes the firmware sealed. **A bad frame costs exactly that frame** — which is why there is a CRC per frame rather than one over the capture — and `seq` makes a lost frame visible as a gap in the sequence.

**Reserved for a future RX direction**: nothing in v1 sends anything to the DUT, but `frame_type` is exactly the field a later command channel is added to, rather than a reshape.

**Host-side outputs**, all three under the study's `streams/` directory:
- `<tap>.bin` — the raw framed stream, written verbatim as it arrives, before any decoding. Always written, even when the build ID does not match, so a mismatch is recoverable rather than a lost run.
- `<tap>.arrival.csv` — `frame_index,rx_utc_ms,frame_bytes`, one row per frame, written incrementally as the bytes arrive. **The trace's clock** (§3 decisions 17, 18). Unrotated on purpose, and `frame_bytes` is what lets the post-hoc join be verified rather than assumed.
- `<tap>.trace.csv` — decoded records, one per row (`frame_index,frame_seq,rx_utc_ms,kind,a,b,name`), with names resolved through the manifest and gaps present as explicit rows. **Written even when no manifest applies**, with the `name` column empty; **and written even when no arrival stamps apply**, with `rx_utc_ms` empty. Both are real states — a timeline of numeric thread pointers is a real answer, and so is an ordered untimed one — and `streams/index.json` carries `named`, `timed` and a prose `note` saying which is which. What is refused is applying the *wrong* manifest, or the *wrong* stamps.

  Consecutive rows repeating one `rx_utc_ms` is the normal case, not a defect: it is a *frame's* stamp. `frame_index` and `frame_seq` both appear because they answer different questions — the index is this capture's own monotonic frame ordinal and is what an arrival stamp is keyed by; the seq is the firmware's own wrapping byte, so frames the wire lost are visible without decoding anything. Note that a jump in either column is *usually a header frame*, which occupies an index and contributes no row — a rendered trace cannot see frame loss, and nothing downstream should claim to.

**That note is readable over HTTP as of 2026-08-26**, and until then it was not — which made it half a mechanism. `GET /study/{id}` returns a `StreamRef` with no room for it and `GET /study/{id}/stream/{name}` serves the rendered CSV either way, so nothing outside Core's own filesystem could tell an unnamed trace from a named one. Closed by a new Core route, `GET /study/{study_id}/streams` ([embarch-core/design.md](../embarch-core/design.md) §3 decision 30(c)'s amendment, §4), which is what [embarch-ui](../embarch-ui/design.md)'s Trace view reads before it renders anything. A view that skipped it would be structurally incapable of the one distinction this paragraph exists to preserve. **That route now carries two booleans rather than one note to read prose from** (§3 decision 18).

**Two independent implementations of this CSV agree, and it is now a test rather than a note.** Under layout 1 this was checked by hand once (2026-08-25, byte-identical in every column of 848 rows bar how many decimals each printed for `us`) — and then went un-rerun across the rework that rewrote *both* decoders, which is precisely how a remembered check rots. `embarch-outpost/tests/cross_decoder.py` now runs with the rest of that repo's suite: it decodes the committed capture with `scripts/decode_outpost.py`, against the same arrival stamps, and diffs the result against the rendering `embarch-core` produced from the same bytes through `embarch-study-designer`'s decoder. **Under layout 2 the two agree on all 941 rows exactly, header line included** — no formatting difference left to excuse, since the `us` column that caused the only layout-1 discrepancy is gone. Verified to *fail* on a one-character divergence rather than assumed to work.

It compares decoder against decoder, which is the check actually available: the firmware encoder is the third implementation and it is the one both decoders are fed *from*. It reads the committed fixtures in the sibling repos rather than a fresh capture — a fresh `native_sim` run carries a different build ID on every dirty tree, and the point is two decoders over **one** set of bytes — and skips loudly rather than failing when those siblings are absent, since DOC-PROTOCOL §2's sibling layout is a convention this repo's own suite should not require.

## 5. Integration into a DUT firmware project

The full path from "this repo exists" to "I am reading a trace." Nothing here is exotic — it is Zephyr's ordinary module mechanism, which is the point.

### 5.1 Getting the module in, and pinning its version

`embarch-outpost` is a Zephyr module: a `zephyr/module.yml` at its root pointing at its own `CMakeLists.txt` and `Kconfig`. A DUT firmware repo consumes it the way `embarch-dev-bench` already consumes `embarch-study-designer` — as a west-managed project (that doc's §3 decision 8) — by adding it to its own `west.yml`:

```yaml
  - name: embarch-outpost
    url: https://github.com/gabrieltetar/embarch-outpost
    revision: v0.1.0          # <- this is where the version lives
    path: modules/embarch-outpost
```

**`revision` is the answer to "how does the outpost version end up in the DUT firmware project."** It is pinned in the consuming repo's manifest, committed alongside that firmware's own history, and updated by a deliberate bump plus `west update` — never floating. ~~A repo that does not own its west manifest (a downstream app in someone else's workspace) uses `ZEPHYR_EXTRA_MODULES` instead; same module, different plumbing.~~

**Corrected 2026-08-26: for anything built through `embarch-api`, the pin is mandatory and the define is unreachable** ([embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 52). `embarch-api`'s `extra_args` are opaque passthrough inserted *before* west's own arguments, so a `--` separator terminates the wrong list — `cmake` receives `-d` as an unknown argument and the build dies before configuring. `ZEPHYR_EXTRA_MODULES` survives only as an escape hatch for a repo built by invoking `west` directly, which is how Phase C validated the module before any manifest entry existed. Pin the module.

A full SHA rather than a tag or branch is what Phase E actually used, and the reason generalises: a floating revision silently changes what the manifest generator resolved for an image **already on the board**, and the whole point of that manifest is that it describes the exact binary flashed.

That pinned revision then shows up in three places that have to agree, which is the property worth having: the module's `git describe` goes into the manifest (§5.3), the firmware emits it in its header record, and a `doctor`-style check can compare the running DUT against the revision currently checked out — the mechanism [embarch-dev-bench/design.md](../embarch-dev-bench/design.md) §3 decision 25 already established for dev-bench firmware.

### 5.2 Turning it on, and declaring the board facts

Three things in the DUT repo, none of them in this one:

**A Kconfig fragment**, kept in a debug-only overlay (`overlay-outpost.conf` or a build type) rather than in `prj.conf`, since this is debug instrumentation and should not be in a shipping image by accident:

```
CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST=y
CONFIG_TRACING_USER=y
```

**Two symbols, not one — corrected 2026-08-25 by trying it** ([embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 38). `CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST` does `select` `TRACING`, `TRACING_ISR`, `THREAD_NAME` and `CRC`. It cannot select `TRACING_USER`, because that is a Kconfig **choice** member and nothing outside a choice can select one: `select` warns and does nothing. Two escape hatches were tried and neither works — `configdefault`, which is resolved after the whole tree is parsed and looked like exactly the mechanism for this, warns and does nothing on a choice symbol; and reopening the choice from the module is impossible, since `Kconfig.zephyr` sources `modules/Kconfig` (line 42) before `subsys/Kconfig` (line 52), so `TRACING_FORMAT_CHOICE` does not exist yet at that point in the parse.

The *property* this was written to guarantee survives: `src/outpost.c` `#error`s without `CONFIG_TRACING_USER`, so the pair still cannot be half-enabled. Only the symbol count was wrong.

**`CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST` also deliberately does not `depends on SERIAL`**, which looks right and creates a genuine Kconfig dependency loop: `SERIAL` is depended on by `SHELL_BACKEND_SERIAL`, which reaches `LOG`, which reaches `SEGGER_SYSTEMVIEW` inside the very `TRACING_FORMAT_CHOICE` this symbol selects `TRACING` to enter. A missing serial driver is caught instead by the `DEVICE_DT_GET` on the chosen node, which cannot compile without one.

**A devicetree `chosen` node**, in the DUT's own board overlay, naming the UART the trace goes out of:

```dts
/ {
    chosen { embarch,outpost-uart = &uart21; };
};
&uart21 {
    status = "okay";
    current-speed = <1000000>;
    pinctrl-0 = <&uart21_default>;   /* the engineer's pins, on the engineer's board */
};
```

This is deliberately the identical shape Zephyr's own tracing backend uses (`DT_CHOSEN(zephyr_tracing_uart)`, `subsys/tracing/tracing_backend_uart.c`) — a convention a Zephyr engineer already knows, not an EmbArch invention. **It is also what removes "which UART instance and pin on the nRF54L15" from this doc's open questions**: it was never this project's fact to hold.

**What a real integration actually looked like, 2026-08-26** (the reference-dut, Milestone 7 Phase E). The three things above are correct and are not the whole job; a Zephyr app of any size already has opinions about the UART you are taking. What that repo needed was a **snippet** (`-S outpost`) carrying the conf fragment and the overlay together, and **three interactions with the app's own build that no amount of reading this doc would have predicted**:

- **The traced UART is the console.** On that board `uart20` carries console, shell, mcumgr and both BT HCI paths, because `SERIAL30` became the PMIC's I²C. So the snippet takes the console off it — and must **not** take the shell off it the obvious way, see below.
- **A snippet that turns the app's shell off can remove the study's only stimulus.** That repo's `ble-shell` snippet moves the shell to BLE NUS, which is what the saved studies write to in order to make the firmware do work. It does that by *re-pointing* Zephyr's serial shell backend at a `zephyr,nus-uart` device — not by bypassing it — so `CONFIG_SHELL_BACKEND_SERIAL=n` disables the NUS service too. It also sets `&uart20 { status = "disabled"; }` to let the fast peripheral domain gate HFCLK, so the outpost's overlay has to be applied **after** it and set the instance back to `okay`. The integration is a composition of two snippets in a fixed order, not a standalone one.
- **The per-instance async symbol can vanish silently.** See §5.3.

The general lesson, and the reason this is in the doc rather than in that repo's notes: **every one of these was found by running a study against the board, and none is visible from either snippet read on its own.** An integration is not done when it compiles.

**Marker declarations**, if the engineer wants spans the kernel cannot see — one registration list in one header, which is simultaneously what makes the macro compile and what generates the manifest's ID → name table (decision 6):

```c
#define OUTPOST_MARKERS(X)      \
    X(PPG_FRAME_BEGIN)          \
    X(PPG_FRAME_END)            \
    X(RADIO_IRQ_BODY)
```

point `CONFIG_EMBARCH_OUTPOST_MARKER_HEADER="app_outpost_markers.h"` at it, and add its directory with **`zephyr_include_directories()`, not `target_include_directories(app ...)`** — the outpost module compiles that header too, and it is not part of the `app` target. Then, anywhere including inside an ISR: `OUTPOST_EVT(PPG_FRAME_BEGIN, frame_no);`

An unregistered name does not compile in either build that has the module: `OUTPOST_EVT` expands through an enumerator that only the registration list creates, and the markers-compiled-out path still references it.

**`<embarch/outpost.h>` is includable in a build without the module, and `OUTPOST_EVT` no-ops there — corrected 2026-08-27 (decision 21).** This is what lets a marker live in shared driver or library code that compiles into both the tracing image and the shipping one, unguarded. The name check degrades in three tiers: module on + markers on emits and checks; module on + markers off no-ops and checks; **module absent no-ops without checking**, because no registration header was included, so there is no enumerator to reference and insisting on one would break every call site in every shipping build. The check stays real in both builds that have the module, which is every build where a marker has a reason to exist.

### 5.3 Kconfig surface

Everything tunable is a Kconfig on the module, so no consuming repo ever edits outpost source. Provisional defaults; §7 is explicit that none of them are measured yet, and building the thing did not change that — a `native_sim` run tells you the pipeline works, not what a real UART sustains.

| Symbol | Default | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| `EMBARCH_OUTPOST` | `n` | Master switch; selects the tracing symbols above |
| `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_BAUD` | `0` | `0` = use the chosen node's `current-speed`; non-zero calls `uart_configure()` at init, so the trace can run faster than a board overlay other things also depend on |
| `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_RING_BYTES` | `4096` | Record ring size — the direct trade against drop rate (decision 5). Divided by `sizeof(struct outpost_slot)`, which is **20** under layout 3, rounded down to a power of two: the default buys 128 slots / 2560 bytes. A build wanting layout 2's 256-slot depth asks for 5120. `BUILD_ASSERT`-pinned, because the divisor has now been wrong in both directions |
| `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_BATCH_BYTES` | `256` | Bytes the drain thread accumulates before a `uart_tx()`. Minimum 192: a batch too small to hold one header frame produces an undecodable stream, so it is a `BUILD_ASSERT` rather than a runtime surprise. **As of layout 2 this is also the instrument's resolution knob** — a frame is the finest interval a trace can place anything in (decision 17), so lowering it buys time resolution and spends wire overhead |
| `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_THREAD_PRIORITY` / `_STACK_SIZE` / `_WAIT_MS` | low / `1024` / `100` | The drain thread. Mirrors `CONFIG_TRACING_THREAD_*`'s own shape |
| `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_TRACE_THREADS` / `_ISRS` / `_IDLE` / `_MARKERS` | `y` | Which hook families emit at all — turning one off is the first lever when the drop rate is too high |
| `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_ISR_IDENTIFY` | `y` on Cortex-M | Read the active vector number (decision 7); off degrades to anonymous ISR enter/exit |
| `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_OVERFLOW_BLOCK` | `n` | Opt into blocking instead of dropping, for a deliberate high-fidelity run (decision 5) |
| `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_HEADER_INTERVAL_MS` | `1000` | How often the header frame repeats, so a host attaching late can still decode. `0` emits it once, at startup |
| `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_UART_ASYNC` | `y` where the driver supports it | `uart_tx()` vs. a `uart_poll_out()` fallback. The fallback spins per byte inside the drain path — present so a port without async support still works, not because it is fine (decision 3). **This symbol being `y` does not mean the async path is compiled in**, and the failure is silent — see below |
| `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_MARKER_HEADER` | `""` | The header declaring this application's `OUTPOST_MARKERS(X)` list. Empty registers no markers |
| `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_BUILD_ID_MAX` | `64` | Longest build-ID string the header frame carries |

**A frame is now a resolution setting, and the table above does not make that obvious.** `BATCH_BYTES` and `THREAD_WAIT_MS` between them decide how often a frame leaves, and therefore how finely the host can place any event in it. Nothing in this repo has measured either against a real UART, so the honest statement is the shape of the trade rather than a number: smaller batches and a shorter wait mean finer placement and more framing overhead per record on a wire that is already the scarce resource, and the balance is a per-board question §7 leaves open.

**The one Kconfig failure mode that produces a working build and the wrong transport, found 2026-08-26** ([embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 51). `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_UART_ASYNC` is a module symbol; whether the *driver* offers the async API for the chosen instance is a separate, per-instance decision. On nrfx, `UART_<n>_ASYNC` depends on `UART_ASYNC_API && !UART_<n>_INTERRUPT_DRIVEN`, and `UART_<n>_INTERRUPT_DRIVEN` is `default y` whenever the global `UART_INTERRUPT_DRIVEN` is on — which any shell-bearing app is likely to turn on for something else entirely. When that happens `UART_<n>_ASYNC` simply **does not appear in `.config`**, no warning is emitted, `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_UART_ASYNC` stays `y`, and the drain thread falls back to the per-byte loop that burns exactly the CPU time the trace exists to measure.

So a consuming repo's fragment needs the per-instance `CONFIG_UART_<n>_INTERRUPT_DRIVEN=n` explicitly, **not** the global one — the global is likely load-bearing for something else. And the integration is not verified by the module's own symbol: read `UART_<n>_ASYNC` out of the generated `.config`.

### 5.4 Build output, and what the engineer never has to do

The module adds a post-link CMake step emitting **`outpost-manifest.json`** into the build directory beside `zephyr.hex` — and **no image patching**, per decision 9's rework. It contains the build ID, the outpost version, the record layout version, the Kconfig cycle rate for reference, the marker ID table, the `_k_thread_obj_*`-plus-kernel thread table (decision 8), the `_sw_isr_table` handler table and its `isr_args` companion (decision 7), a content-fingerprint CRC, and a `notes` array saying what could **not** be resolved and why. The generator never fills a gap with a plausible answer; it records the gap.

**The engineer never handles that file.** `embarch-api`'s `build`/`build_and_flash` picks it up and hands it to Core alongside the artifact ([embarch-api/design.md](../embarch-api/design.md) §3 decision 39) — because the failure mode of forgetting is not a visible error but a silently mislabelled trace, and the only reliable fix is for the manifest to travel automatically with the build that produced it. That is why the implementation derives the manifest's path from the artifact's own directory rather than accepting it as a parameter: a parameter is a thing a dozen call sites can forget.

**Two build-system facts found writing this step, both of which produce a wrong answer rather than an error.** Zephyr's `extra_post_build_commands` hook fires on the `zephyr.elf` rule — which on a `native_sim` build is a **relocatable object whose symbol addresses are all zero**, upstream of the host link that produces the real image. Reading it yields a manifest full of zeroes and a successful build. And the names of both the final target and the final file (`logical_target_for_zephyr_elf`, `BYPRODUCT_KERNEL_*_NAME`) are set in `zephyr/CMakeLists.txt` *after* module CMakeLists run, so neither exists to be referenced at module scope. The step therefore defers to the end of Zephyr's own directory scope, where both are available and `native_runner_executable` can be distinguished from an ordinary ELF target.

### 5.5 The whole loop, once

Per bench, once: declare the signal's route in `embarch-ui`'s Topology tab ([embarch-ui/design.md](../embarch-ui/design.md) §3 decision 10) — `direct`, plus the USB bridge's serial.

Per firmware repo, once: the four steps in §5.1/§5.2.

Per study, thereafter: add an `outpost` tap to the `Study`, run it, read the trace. `west update`s and rebuilds carry the manifest along on their own.

## 6. Relationship to existing sub-projects

- **[embarch-topology](../embarch-topology/design.md)** — owns the new DUT-signal-link entity and its route (§3 decision 11; that doc's §3 decision 18). It is the only place that knows whether `outpost` currently arrives via a USB bridge on the Core machine or via dev-bench, and the only place a human declares it.
- **[embarch-core](../embarch-core/design.md)** — **is the trace's clock** as of layout 2 (§3 decisions 17, 18): it stamps every frame with its own receipt time into `streams/<tap>.arrival.csv` and joins those stamps onto the rows at render time, refusing rather than shifting when the join cannot be verified. It also opens the tap for the study's duration, writes `streams/`, stores the manifest the study's own flash bound to it, decodes against that manifest, and serves the results. Core is the only process that touches the serial port, exactly as it is for the dev-bench link and for `/serial-log`. **All of it is built as of 2026-08-26** (that doc's §3 decisions 30/31, Milestone 7 Phase B item 1 and Phase C's own trigger): the tap opens a `Route::Direct` signal's own port, `streams/` is written raw-first, `POST /flash` carries the manifest, Core holds it per chip and clears it when that chip is flashed without one, a study snapshots the binding at start, and the trace is decoded post-hoc against it — with a build-ID mismatch costing the names and never the capture. **[embarch-api](../embarch-api/design.md)'s pickup-and-send half shipped in the same pass**, since one trigger governed both.
- **[embarch-study-designer](../embarch-study-designer/design.md)** — owns `StreamTap` and the accepted inbound pipeline types (§3 decision 13; that doc's §3 decision 39). The outpost adds one `StreamSource` variant and one `StreamEncoding` variant; it does not get its own parallel type family. **It also owns the trace's own types and row shape** as of 2026-08-25 (`src/outpost.rs`) — the record and header types, the frame decoder, the manifest type and the `*.trace.csv` columns (the cycle unwrapper was there too, and layout 2 deleted it: nothing on this wire wraps any more) — for the same reason `Sample` and `GattTranscriptEntry` live there: Core holds no column knowledge, and `embarch-ui` will read a trace through the same code Core writes it with rather than a second implementation. **This is the one such type family that is not a dev-bench wire contract** (decision 11 has the bench interpreting nothing), so no C decoder mirrors it and no both-languages pin applies; the mirror that does exist is `scripts/decode_outpost.py` and the firmware encoder itself.
- **[embarch-dev-bench](../embarch-dev-bench/design.md)** — no work in v1, and that is the point: the bypass route exists so the outpost is not blocked on bench hardware. Gains a pass-through role only when the `via-dev-bench` route becomes real.
- **[embarch-api](../embarch-api/design.md)** — carries the manifest from the build to Core alongside the firmware artifact (**built 2026-08-26**, by deriving it from the artifact's own directory rather than from a parameter), and exposes the trace to agents through `study_stream_data`, which already serves any declared tap by name and needed no outpost-specific work.
- **[embarch-ui](../embarch-ui/design.md)** — two surfaces: the Topology tab is where a signal's route is declared (§3 decision 11), and a trace view renders the recorded timeline. That view carries layout 2's honesty burden concretely (that doc's §3 decision 10): it draws an axis of milliseconds only when *every* frame is stamped, falls back to frame indices otherwise, and excludes a span that lives inside one frame from every total while still counting it.
- **[embarch-promptu](../embarch-promptu/design.md)** — eventually the natural home for "read this trace and tell me where the time went," but nothing here depends on it.

## 7. Open questions

- ~~**Every wire constant's *value***~~ — **four of them are measured now, and the two that mattered most were not the ones this bullet expected.** The Kconfig defaults in §5.3 were all unmeasured guesses, and this bullet predicted the first real UART would mostly be about baud and batch size. What a day of measuring on an `dut_dev@7` actually settled:

  | knob | was | is | why |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | `current-speed` (DUT overlay) | 230400 | **460800** | 230400 is 23040 B/s and the tracer sustained ~22.9 KB/s of it *while the DUT was idle* |
  | `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_TRACE_SELF` | (did not exist) | **n** | 50.4% of the reference capture was the instrument describing its own transmission (§3 decision 19) |
  | `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_FILL_WAIT_MS` | (did not exist) | **10** | frames had settled at 3.3 records, so ~20% of the link was framing overhead (§3 decision 20) |
  | `EMBARCH_OUTPOST_TRACE_IDLE` | y | **n** *in the reference-dut's snippet* | 5292 `idle` records against 5052 idle-thread switch-ins — the same fact on the wire twice, and `embarch-ui` never adds the second to a total |

  **The baud has a ceiling and it is 460800, which is a property of this bench and not of anything designed here.** Measured against the port directly, with no Core in the path: the MCP2200 bridge **refuses to open above 750000** (921600 and 1000000 both fail with "a device attached to the system is not functioning"; 750000 and below open and carry bytes), and the nRF54L15's UARTE has **no baud between 460800 and 921600** (`uart_nrfx_uarte.c`'s `NRF_UARTE_BAUDRATE` macro is a fixed ladder, so 750000 is not a rate this SoC can produce). The two constraints intersect at exactly one useful value. Note what this cost: 1000000 is `embarch-core`'s own `EMBARCH_SIGNAL_BAUD` default, so the obvious answer would have needed no Core-side setting at all.

  **Bytes per record was the next thing this bullet named, and 2026-08-27 evening deferred it — because the number that justified it turned out to be a measurement of a broken system.** After all four changes a record costs ~10 bytes on the wire, and the two largest contributors are both absolute 32-bit values encoded as five-byte varints — `cycles`, and `a` when it is a RAM pointer. A per-record delta on `cycles` was rejected in §3 decision 4, correctly, *for a reason that was about emit-path cost and not about wire cost*; the wire-cost argument was never the question then.

  **The case for acting was "4141 rec/s delivered at 97% duty plus 5452 lost across 113 gaps" — roughly 4700 rec/s more than 460800 can carry. That capture's BLE link was in the failed state.** The first traced capture taken with the link actually holding (PPG flowing, the full two-minute drain, 140 s) measures **1606 rec/s at 10.66 B/record** — about 17 KB/s against this link's ~46 KB/s, with **1.12% frame loss**. Roughly 2.5× headroom, not a 2× shortfall. The PPG path itself is what differs: `g_AFE_DRAIN_WORK_Q` ran ~500 times/s on the failing run and **36 times/s** on the healthy one. So the requirement was derived from a capture of a system whose link was dying ([embarch-dev-bench/design.md](../embarch-dev-bench/design.md) §4 finding 3, [embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 96).

  **Deferred, with the target and the trigger both written down so neither has to be re-derived.** Target if it resumes: base-relative `a` (subtract the RAM origin — 5 bytes to 3 on a thread pointer) plus a delta on `cycles`, plausibly ~6 B/record and a ~7500 rec/s ceiling. Cost: firmware, `embarch-study-designer`'s decoder, `embarch-core`, the Python reference decoder, the committed fixtures, the cross-decoder test, and a version byte to 4. **Trigger: a healthy capture that actually runs short of the link** — measured on a link that is holding, which is the condition the old number failed. Every cheaper lever is genuinely spent (instrument self-excluded, idle and GPIO off, batch paced, baud at the measured ceiling, ring at 2048 slots and the build at 81.75% of 256 KB), so this remains the next lever; it is simply not one the evidence currently asks for.

- ~~**Which UART instance and pin on the nRF54L15, and which USB-UART bridge.**~~ **Both resolved.** The instance and pin were never this project's question — declared by a `chosen { embarch,outpost-uart = ... }` node and its `current-speed` in the DUT repo's own board overlay (§5.2), the same shape Zephyr's own tracing backend uses; baud, ring size and every other tunable are Kconfigs on the module (§5.3). ~~**The bridge half resolved 2026-08-25 (§3 decision 16): there is no bridge.**~~ **Reopened 2026-08-26 by the bench, and it is the one thing blocking Phase E.** Decision 16's amendment has the evidence: the DUT's trace UART reaches the host over no port at all — the J-Link's own VCOM on this DUT's debug connector returns zero bytes with a *proven-running* console build on the board. **Closed 2026-08-27.** A bridge is needed and one was already clipped on: an MCP2200 on the `dut_dev@7` DBG header, whose uart20 is **TX P1.05 / RX P1.04** (schematic `DBG_TX`/`DBG_RX`, nets crossed on rev 6 and 7). This bullet spent a day naming P1.00 instead, because the build had been targeting `roadrunner@2` rather than the board on the bench — rows 83–85. The third-device disambiguation hazard is real and resolved: the MCP2200 reports no USB serial at all, so Core enumerates it as `"5"` from a hub-location path (row 82), and it sits on the bench beside dev-bench's ESP32 CDC and the DUT's J-Link VCOM. The third-device disambiguation hazard this bullet originally feared comes back with it, and is now concrete: an MCP2200 bridge is already enumerated on this bench alongside dev-bench's CP2102N and the DUT's J-Link.
- ~~**The cycle counter's actual rate and wrap period on the nRF54L15.**~~ **Moot as of 2026-08-26: there is no cycle counter on this wire.** It was answered as a config read on 2026-08-25 — 1 MHz, ~71.6-minute wrap — and then §3 decision 4's rework removed every timestamp from the record, which took the rate, the wrap, the unwrapper and [embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 40's whole failure mode with it. **What replaces this bullet is a different measurement, and it is now the instrument's headline spec:** how many milliseconds a frame actually takes to arrive at Core, and how much that jitters. A frame is the resolution of everything the trace says (decision 17), and nothing has measured it — a USB-serial bridge's latency timer is commonly ~16 ms, OS buffering is real, and both land directly on this number.
- ~~**Whether `run_study` gains a flash step.**~~ **Resolved 2026-08-25** — [embarch-study-designer/design.md](../embarch-study-designer/design.md) §3 decision 40: a `Study` declares the versions it requires, and reflash is the operator's per-run choice (`embarch-api/design.md` §3 decision 40). **What stays open is narrower and worth watching**: reflash defaults to off, so the common path still leans on the compile-time build ID alone, with the dirty-tree blind spot decision 9 names. Whether that bites in practice is a question only real use answers.
- **Whether the manifest reaches a Core running on another machine.** It rides the firmware-artifact path, which inherits [embarch-api/design.md](../embarch-api/design.md) §9's known artifact-transfer gap for a remote Core. Same gap, not a new one — but the outpost makes it bite in a second place.
- ~~**What the trace view actually looks like.**~~ **Built 2026-08-26 against the closest thing to a real capture that exists, and stated as such** ([embarch-ui/design.md](../embarch-ui/design.md) §3 decision 10). A lane-per-subject timeline — one lane per thread pointer, one for CPU idle, one per interrupt vector — with markers as ticks and every gap drawn as a band. Designed against the committed `native_sim` capture, which is the firmware encoder's own output and **not a DUT's**. Two things that data changed directly: markers went from full-height rules to ticks plus a faint rule, because they swamped every span on the chart; and a gap band turned out **not** to be an empty interval, so it is drawn as an overlay over what survived rather than as a hole.

  **The load summary is built too**, and §3 decision 4's rework reshaped what it can say. Per subject: entries, measured spans, total extent and share of the window, with **four** classes of span excluded from a total and counted separately — crossing a gap, no closing record, no opening record, and *inside a single frame*, that last one new. Two findings worth keeping: **idle is reported twice by construction**, once as `Idle` records and once as switches of the thread the manifest names `idle`, so summing them claimed nearly twice the window ([embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 53); and **389 of the committed capture's spans, including every ISR span, live inside one frame**, so the view reports ISR *entries* and refuses to report ISR *time* (decision 17).

  Rendering the tab in a browser rather than only through its Rust tests also found a shipped defect nothing else could have: the Load button and the load table's `<tbody>` both had `id="trace-load"`, so `getElementById` returned the button and **every summary row had been rendering into it.** The load table had never once displayed. Fixed the same pass; recorded here because a Rust-tested view that has never been looked at is exactly the shape of thing that hides this.

  **Still open in the part that matters:** this view has rendered a simulator's capture and never a DUT's, it has never rendered *real* arrival stamps (the committed stamped fixture is synthesised 20 ms pacing over real frames, said so in its own test), and §5.3's constants remain unmeasured. A working view is not validation of the numbers under it.

- **Most of a real DUT's threads render as raw pointers, and that may matter more in practice than it reads here.** Decision 8 states the limit — a `k_thread_create()` thread into a plain `static struct k_thread` has no distinguishing symbol — and the reference-dut is the first case where its *scale* is visible: the manifest names 5 threads out of a build that runs considerably more. The stated fix (declare it with `K_THREAD_DEFINE`, or mark it) is real but is a change to someone else's firmware. Whether that is an acceptable ask or whether DWARF type inspection eventually has to happen is a question only using the trace answers.

  **The rendering half is answered as of 2026-08-26; the readability half is not, and Phase E did not get to answer it either** — the first real capture has not happened (§7's hardware bullet), so the question of whether a reference-dut trace whose majority of lanes are hex is *usable* is still open, and still not one a simulator capture can answer. One thing Phase E did establish about it, from building the real image rather than from reasoning: the outpost's **own** drain thread — the one whose priority-10 behaviour is the thing an engineer would most want to find in the trace — calls `k_thread_name_set(&drain_thread, "outpost")`, so it names itself **at runtime on the wire** even though the static manifest cannot resolve `&drain_thread` (a plain `static struct k_thread`, exactly decision 8's stated limit). The proportion of hex lanes is therefore slightly better than the manifest's 5-of-many suggests, and the specific lane most worth reading is not one of them.

  **The rendering half, as built:** [embarch-ui](../embarch-ui/design.md)'s Trace view treats an unnamed subject as a first-class state rather than an error path — italic, dotted, labelled with the pointer it actually has — and three of the seven thread pointers in the committed `native_sim` capture exercise that path, along with every ISR record in it (that ELF has no `_sw_isr_table` at all, so all of them report `IRQ_UNKNOWN` and the lane says the vector was not reported rather than inventing one). So a view built against real proportions does not *look* broken. Whether a reference-dut trace where the majority of lanes are hex is actually **usable** is still an open question, and it is not one a simulator capture can answer.
- ~~**Whether the anonymous interrupt-load total is enough in practice.**~~ **Resolved by revisiting decision 7 the same session** — vector numbers are read directly and resolved against `_sw_isr_table`, so ISRs are named without instrumentation. ~~What replaces it is narrower and real: the build variants that defeat that resolution.~~ **Checked 2026-08-25, and the answer was better and worse than expected.** None of `CONFIG_DYNAMIC_INTERRUPTS`, multi-level aggregator offsets or `ARM_CUSTOM_INTERRUPT_CONTROLLER` is present in the reference-dut build — but a variant this doc never named, Nordic's shared `nrfx_isr` trampoline, defeats 8 of its 13 IRQs, and the fix is implemented (§3 decision 7, [embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 42). **What is still open is narrower again**: `CONFIG_SHARED_INTERRUPTS` would put a `z_shared_isr` dispatcher in the table position with no equivalent `arg` recovery, and no build in this suite has it on, so nothing has exercised that path.
- **A burst still overruns the ring under real study load, and the only lever left is the record's size. The binding number is 9.92 bytes.**

  Measured on the demo capture — BLE connect, L4 pairing, GATT discovery, PPG measurement running: **1651 records/s at 36% link duty, and 6157 records lost in 3 gaps, 19.7% of everything generated.** A later run that got further into the same study, with PPG data actually flowing after `sensor01 start`, is worse and is the number to plan against: **4141 records/s delivered at 97% duty, plus 5452 lost across 113 gaps** — the DUT wants roughly 4700 records/s more than 460800 can carry, so on the real demo workload this link is saturated and the losses are continuous rather than bursty. Each gap lost almost exactly 2048 records, which is the ring's whole depth: a burst fills it and then keeps dropping until the drain catches up. Average capacity is nowhere near the constraint; the drain thread's **peak** throughput is, and that is `link rate ÷ bytes per record` — 46080 ÷ 9.92 ≈ **4600 records/s**.

  Everything cheaper has been tried and is in: the instrument is out of its own trace, idle and GPIO records are off, the batch is paced, the baud is at the link's ceiling, and the ring has gone 128 → 512 → 2048 slots. The ring cannot go further on this DUT — 2048 slots is already 40 KB and the debug build sits at 81.75% of 256 KB.

  So the next lever is **bytes per record**, and it is a layout-4 change. Of the 9.92, the two largest contributors are both absolute 32-bit values in five-byte varints: `cycles`, and `a` when it is a RAM pointer. §3 decision 4 rejected a per-record delta on `cycles` **for a reason that was entirely about emit-path cost** — "it does not address the stated problem: the emit path still reads the counter" — and that reasoning is still correct and no longer relevant, because the stated problem is now the wire. A delta on `cycles` plus a base-relative `a` would plausibly take a record to ~6 bytes and the peak to ~7500 records/s. It costs the firmware, both host decoders, the fixtures and a version-byte bump, and it is **not** made here.

  Until it is, the honest position is that this capture drops records in bursts and **says so**: three gap bands, each one bounded by the firmware's own `cycles`/`b` pair, with every span crossing them flagged `crosses_gap` and excluded from every total. That is decision 5 working as designed, not a silent loss — but it is a real limit on what this trace can claim about a busy interval.

- **Overhead of the instrumentation itself — the CPU half is now measured; the emit-path half still is not, and the two got confused for a day.** Layout 3 restored the DUT clock, so the trace **can** be the instrument for part of its own overhead after all, and the answer on a real reference-dut is: the outpost's drain thread is **1.6% of wall time** (66.3 ms over 778 runs, 85 µs each) and every ISR in the system together is **0.6%**. That was worth knowing and it was not what the trace *appeared* to say — `embarch-ui` reported the drain thread at 78% while it was still timing spans by frame arrival ([embarch-decision-reversals.md](../embarch-decision-reversals.md) row 86). **Both numbers came out of the same capture; only one of them came out of the right clock.**

  What is still unmeasured is the part that matters most and the trace cannot see: **the cost the hooks impose on the code they observe.** 85 µs per drain-thread run is the drain thread's own work, not the per-hook cost charged to an unrelated context switch. The honest check is unchanged in shape and still not run: the same study against a build with the outpost compiled out and one with it in. Nothing in the stream can substitute for it, because every record is emitted *by* the path being measured. That still needs a marker pair around a known-length loop, a GPIO toggle on a scope, or a `k_cycle_get_32()` read in the *application's* own code, which is the engineer's to add and not this module's to provide.

  Decision 4's rework was itself a bet on this number — that a counter read inside the context switch and inside `_isr_wrapper()` is a cost worth removing — made without a measurement, at the owner's call. Worth recording plainly, and now with a twist: **the change that removed the ability to measure emit-path overhead was motivated by emit-path overhead**, and its own reversal a day later is what made the *other* half of the overhead question answerable. If the trade ever needs revisiting, the layout version is the mechanism, and the measurement above is the evidence to get first.
